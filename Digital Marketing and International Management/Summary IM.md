## Bigger picture
### Megatrends
***Megatrends*** are durable, directional structural forces that reshape the conditions under which societies, individuals, and organisations across the world operate. They are historically specific rather than permanent features of human societies, **resilient to individual action and short-term disruption**, and grounded in dynamics strong enough to **persist for decades** (they shape something long term).
- Acceleration of **technology disruption** and the digital economy; energy transition and sustainable innovation
- Large **demographic shifts** (urbanization, population changes); increased threats for health, new diseases, aging; growing economic inequalities
- Worsening **climate change** impact and rising resource scarcity
- Rising **social instability**
- **Fracturing world**: political polarization

### Navigating the "Globalization Yo-Yo" (P. Ghemawat)
Business leaders face a dangerous pattern: **expectations about globalization swing wildly between extremes** — from "the world is flat" euphoria (Friedman) to "globalization is dead" despair (2008–2012), to geopolitical turmoil — creating costly strategic mistakes…
![[Lessons-1791187796457.webp]]

The 3 phases of the yo-yo:
1. **Euphoria phase ("flat world")** — overestimation of global integration → standardized strategies that ignore local differences
2. **Reality check (Lehmann)** — shocks like financial crises or political changes reveal the limits of globalization
3. **Panic phase (recent days)** — overreaction toward localization, abandoning valuable global scale and connections, plus trade wars

> **Triple A (Ghemawat):** **Adaptation** (local responsiveness), **Aggregation** (global scale/economies), **Arbitrage** (absolute economies, exploiting differences)

Coca-Cola has been bouncing between local and global actor with the change of the management.
![[Lessons-1791470264827.webp]]

**Globalization and the "yo-yo" effect — a global perspective for firms** (professor's slide):

| Macro globalization expectations | Strategy: how to compete | Presence: where to compete | Architecture: how to connect | Nonmarket strategy: anger and its management |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **High** | Replicable solutions / build scale (**aggregation**) | Growth opportunity / premium | Centralization | Global actor |
| ⇕ | ⇕ | ⇕ | ⇕ | ⇕ |

When expectations about globalization shift up or down, *all* strategic levers shift accordingly (scale ↔ localization, premium ↔ risk, centralization ↔ decentralization, global actor ↔ local citizen).

## What Is Globalisation
Globalization refers to the **shift toward a more integrated and interdependent world economy.**
> anche se cambiamo città troviamo più o meno gli stessi negozi.

This long-term and multifaceted phenomenon involves the increasing interconnectedness of countries through various exchanges: goods, services, technology, information, trade, financial flows, and people.

*(from our economic/engineering standpoint)* It primarily includes two major dynamics: the **globalisation of markets** and the **globalisation of production**.

### Globalization of Markets
Globalization of markets refers to the **merging of historically distinct national markets into one vast global marketplace**. Consumer tastes and preferences around the world are converging, making it possible for firms to sell increasingly standardised products globally – but it is far from being that simple…

This converging trend is driven by factors such as improved communication technologies, global media, and increased ease of travel, which expose consumers worldwide to similar products and lifestyles.

| Shop | Approach | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coca-Cola | A soft drink sold with a largely consistent formula and branding worldwide | different flavours between Italy and USA |
| McDonald's | Standardised fast-food experience and core menu items across international operations, plus a set of extra local offerings | e.g. localized menus |
| Apple | iPhones, devices and software platforms quite identical in form and function globally, with minor regional adaptations | |
| Nike | Athletic footwear and apparel with universal designs and branding | |

The globalization of markets presents significant opportunities for companies to expand their reach and achieve **economies of scale**, while also posing challenges related to local preferences, cultures, competitive dynamics, etc.

### Globalization of Production
The globalization of production refers to the **sourcing of goods and services from optimal/optimized locations around the globe**, to leverage national differences in factors of production (labour, energy, land, capital), i.e. regarding quality and costs. This allows companies to take advantage of cost differences, specialized skills, and resource availability across countries, enhancing efficiency and competitiveness.

| Example | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Boeing 787 Dreamliner** | 65% outsourced… wings from Japan, fuselage sections from Italy, engines from UK. Final assembly in the US |
| **Nike shoes** | Designs created in Oregon, manufacturing in Vietnam, Indonesia, China, etc., benefitting from lower labor costs and substantial availability of labor |
| **Automotive industry** | Car parts sourced globally, then assembled. US automotive industry totaled ~90 BlnUSD sourcing in 2024, Top 10 sourcing countries: Mexico, Canada, China, Japan, South Korea, Germany, India, Taiwan |
| **SW development** | Coding in India, design in California, testing in Eastern EU — optimizing expertise, costs, etc. |

While this approach creates complex and impacting global supply chains, it enables companies to optimize costs and efficiencies, and access specialized capabilities worldwide.

### Key institutions supporting Globalization
As markets globalise and business increasingly crosses borders, several institutions have emerged to manage and regulate the global marketplace.
![[Lessons-1791471528519.webp]]
> These institutions, created through voluntary agreements between nations, provide the governance infrastructure that should enable globalization to function effectively. Other global institutions such as **G7, G20 or OECD** (OCSE in Italian) are not mentioned here, but extremely relevant as well.

- **WTO** (from GATT): monitors the global trading system, ensures member states adhere to negotiated trade rules. 164 nations, 98% of world trade → enormous influence in reducing trade barriers.
- **World Bank**: promotes economic development, primarily through low-interest loans to developing nations for infrastructure projects.
- **IMF** (1944): maintains order in the international monetary system; often lender of last resort to countries in turmoil, with conditions aimed at economic stability.
- **UN** (1945): promotes international cooperation on peace, human rights, development. ~70% of UN activities focus on economic progress and social development.

### The Primary Drivers of Globalization
**Declining Trade and Investment Barriers**
Trump (and Brexit) aside, since World War II advanced industrial nations have progressively reduced barriers to cross-border movement of goods, services and capital:
- GATT/WTO negotiations lowered average tariff rates from **over 40% in 1950 to about 2–3% today** in developed nations (before Trump47)
- Bilateral and regional agreements (EU, NAFTA, etc.) further reduced trade barriers
- Most changes to national laws governing foreign direct investment (FDI) since 2000 have largely created more favourable conditions

**Technological Change**
Advancements in technology have significantly reduced former geographic constraints:
- *Communications*: microprocessors, satellites, optical fibre, wireless technologies…
- *Internet*: a global information backbone with multiple billion users (**68% of global population** according to ITU, end of 2024)
- *Transportation*: commercial jet aircraft, containerisation, super freighters reducing shipping time and costs
![[Lessons-1791472273779.webp]]
WTO data (2025): merchandise trade volume (index 2019=100) collapsed in 2020, recovered after; world trade volume growth ≈ **−1.0% (2023), +2.9% (2024)**, projections **+0.9% (2025), +1.8% (2026)**, with Asia the main positive contributor by region.

Implications of Tech change for Globalization:

| For Markets | For Production |
| --- | --- |
| Creates (electronic) global marketplaces | Enables firms to coordinate globally dispersed production systems |
| Reduces the cultural distance between countries | Makes outsourcing of manufacturing and services more viable |
| Enables global media to create shared consumer experiences | Allows businesses to locate activities in optimal locations worldwide |
| Facilitates the emergence of global brands and global consumers | Facilitates just-in-time inventory systems across borders |
| Makes market entry possible for smaller firms | Enables 24-hour work cycles using time zone differences |

### Shift in Foreign Direct Investment and World Order
At least two significant trends have emerged in cross-border investment flows, proving the growth of internationalization patterns:
1. **Sustained growth in FDI since 1990**, with periodic fluctuations due to global economic conditions (1990? → USSR; Berlin Wall…)
2. **Increasing importance of developing nations as investment destinations**

China has been the largest recipient among developing nations, receiving approximately **250 billion USD in inflows (2020)**. This sustained flow of foreign investment into developing nations is a key stimulus for economic growth in the **BRICS** countries and alike.

- **1960s:** global business dominated by large US multinationals; US firms ≈ two-thirds of FDI; British, Dutch, French firms also prominent

### The "Law of Semi-globalization" (P. Ghemawat)
- **~25% is the level of globalization we've achieved** on the planet
- experts **overestimate it by a factor of 5**
![[Lessons-1791472821638.webp]]
> "International interactions, while significant, are **much less intense than domestic interactions**."
> Across all types of flows — trade, capital, information, people — the **average depth ratio is around 25%**:
> - **29% Global Trade** — gross exports as % of world GDP (2024, World Bank): goods/production ≈ 40%, services ≈ 15%
> - **11% Multinational Output** — foreign operations' share of global production
> - **14% Internet Traffic** — cross-border share of total internet activity
>
> National borders still represent very high barriers, but international flows are too significant for companies to focus exclusively on domestic markets.

### The "Law of Distance" (P. Ghemawat) — the CAGE framework
![[Lessons-1791473090660.webp]]
> "International interactions are **dampened by distance** along cultural, administrative, geographic, and economic dimensions" — the **CAGE** framework.
- **Cultural distance:** different languages, religions, and values reduce interactions. Common language **doubles** trade and FDI flows.
- **Administrative distance:** political hostility, lack of trade agreements, different currencies create barriers. Colonial ties boost flows by a factor of **2.5**.
- **Geographic distance:** physical distance remains powerful. **Halving distance more than triples trade and doubles FDI.**
- **Economic distance:** differences in wealth/income levels and in the cost/quality of resources (labour, capital) shape flows — rich countries trade disproportionately with other rich countries.

### The "Globaloney Problem" (P. Ghemawat)
According to surveys and research, most managers consistently **overestimate globalization levels by five times** on average. This "globaloney" leads to costly business mistakes and fuels anti-globalization sentiment.

| Common Misconceptions | Reality Check |
| --- | --- |
| Truly global companies should compete everywhere | Less than 0.1% of firms have foreign operations |
| Competing the same way everywhere is optimal | Only ~1% of firms export |
| Global companies should have no home base | Median US multinational operates in just 3 countries |
| Globalization offers limitless growth | Distance effects persist despite technology |

These biases stem from various sorts of bias and misconceptions — the fantasy that new technology abolishes borders and distances.
**Stress tests** for these empirical laws: 1929 crunch, 2008, Trump tariffs…
So companies should **leverage their natural advantages and closest relationships**.

![[Lessons-1791473292043.webp]]
Proving the laws (2000–2024): average distance of world goods trade **rose** from ~4,400 km to ~5,000 km, while the % of trade happening **within regions fell** from >56% to ~51% → trade is neither fully global nor local, but **semiglobalized**.

![[Lessons-1791473385009.webp]]
**The state of globalization — DHL Global Connectedness Index** (183 countries): despite headlines proclaiming globalization's crises, the index shows only bumps in 2008 and 2020 and a basically **stable value around 25% since the COVID crisis**. By component: **information** flows grew to ~40%, trade and capital ~20–30%, while **people** flows remain minimal (~2%).

## The Global Economy of the 21st Century
![[Lessons-1791473781587.webp]]
**Where the global economy stands — drivers and risks:**
- **Falling barriers:** trade and investment barriers have generally declined, though recent developments (higher tariffs under the Trump administration) represent potential counter-trends
- **Rising economies:** more nations joining the ranks of developed economies (e.g. South Korea and Taiwan), transforming from developing nations to hosts of major global firms
- **Policy shifts:** widespread adoption of liberal economic policies by countries that had firmly opposed them, though with some reversals (e.g. Russia; US–China trade war)

**Greater risks:**
- **Financial contagion and domino effects:** 1997–1998 Asian financial crisis (spread to Russia and Brazil); 2008–2009 global financial crisis (from the US housing market)
- **Supply chain vulnerabilities:** disruptions in one region can affect production globally
- **Increased competition:** firms face rivals from around the world
- **Political backlash:** rising fragmentation and nationalism in multiple countries threatens globalization trends
- **AI evolutions and disruptions:** still hard to analyze and place into perspective, regarding regulations (or lack thereof), usage and competitive implications
