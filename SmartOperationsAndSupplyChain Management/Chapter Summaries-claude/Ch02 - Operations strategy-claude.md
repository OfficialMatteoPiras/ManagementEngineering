# Chapter 2 – Operations and strategic impact

> [!abstract] In one breath (for the oral)
> Operations strategy must satisfy **stakeholders** (and CSR), state a **vision** of how operations contribute (Hayes & Wheelwright, stages 1→4), and be built from **four perspectives**: **top-down** (reflect business strategy, with correspondence and coherence; business model vs operating model), **outside-in** (market requirements: performance objectives, order-winners vs qualifiers, life cycle), **bottom-up** (learn from experience) and **inside-out** (build capabilities). The perspectives are reconciled with the **operations strategy matrix** and, over time, the **line of fit**. Trade-offs are visualised with the **efficient frontier**.

## 2.1 Stakeholders and corporate social responsibility (CSR)

> [!PDF|red] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=70|p.70]]
> All operations have stakeholders. They are the people and groups who have a legitimate interest in the operation's strategy. Some are internal (employees); others are external (customers, society or community groups, and a company's shareholders). […] in any kind of organisation, it is a responsibility of the operations function to understand the (often conflicting) objectives of its stakeholders and set its objectives accordingly.

**CSR** is closely related to the stakeholder view: listening and responding to the needs of a company's stakeholders, including sustainable development, and building good relationships with employees, suppliers and wider society.

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791193646521.webp]]

> [!info] 📖 Table 2.1 – Typical stakeholders' performance objectives · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=71|p.71]]
> Read it as a **two-way exchange**: for each group, *what it wants from the operation* vs *what the operation wants from it*. For example:
> - **Shareholders** want return, stable earnings and liquidity; they give investment capital and long-term commitment.
> - **Staff** want fair wages, safety and development; they give attendance, effort, honesty and engagement.
> - **Suppliers** want early notice of requirements, long-term orders and on-time payment; they give integrity of delivery, innovation and price reductions.
> - **Regulators, government, lobby groups, society:** conformance to rules and minimising negative effects (noise, traffic), in exchange for consistency, low and simple taxation, infrastructure and support.
> **Takeaway:** the objectives often **conflict** (shareholders vs staff vs society), and operations has to balance them.

> [!important] OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE
> Operations strategy should take the requirements of significant stakeholders into account. ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=70|p.70]])

## 2.2 The vision of operations' contribution: Hayes & Wheelwright's Four-Stage Model

| Stage | Name | Vision | In plain words |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Internally neutral** | Avoid the big mistakes | Operations "holds the organisation back"; its goal is to be ignored |
| 2 | **Externally neutral** | Be as good as competitors | Copy **best practice** of the industry leaders; follow, don't set trends |
| 3 | **Internally supportive** | Be clearly the best in the industry | Link operations to the company's strategic goals and develop the resources to excel where the company competes |
| 4 | **Externally supportive** | Give an operations-based competitive advantage | Operations look long-term and build tomorrow's capabilities. **Operations drive strategy** |

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791193952041.webp]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 2.2 – Hayes & Wheelwright's Four-Stage Model · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=73|p.73]]
> - **X axis:** increasing operations *capabilities*. **Y axis:** increasing *strategic impact*, from "holding the organisation back" up to "redefining industry expectations".
> - The staircase shows the change in role: Stage 1 *corrects the worst problems* (implementing strategy) → Stage 2 *adopts best practice* → Stage 3 *links strategy with operations* (supporting strategy) → Stage 4 *gives an operations advantage* (**driving** strategy).
> - **How to explain it:** "Operations move from *implementing* strategy, to *supporting* it, to *driving* it."

🎓 Stage 4 examples from class: **IKEA**, **Toyota** (TPS, the Toyota Production System), **Southwest Airlines** (invented the low-cost flying model that others like Ryanair then copied). Vinelli calls it "the apotheosis of the operations manager": operations *make* the strategy.

### The four perspectives on operations strategy

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791376583944.webp]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 2.3 – The four perspectives on operations strategy · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=74|p.74]]
> Operations strategy sits in the middle and is pulled from four directions:
> - **Top-down:** reflect what the whole group or business wants to do.
> - **Outside-in:** translate the intended market position into objectives for operations.
> - **Bottom-up:** learn from day-to-day activities and build strategic capabilities cumulatively.
> - **Inside-out:** develop resources and processes whose capabilities can be exploited in the chosen markets.
> **None of the four alone is enough**: the strategy is where they meet.

### Context, content and process of operations strategy

The distinction was popularised by **Andrew Pettigrew** (an eight-year study of a single firm):
- **Context:** the circumstances in which the strategy is formed, internal (structure, culture, skills) and external (markets, geopolitics, disruptions, rivals). "What should we do? *It depends.*"
- **Content:** the actual strategic decisions that shape the long-term direction (capacity, supply network, technology…).
- **Process:** *how* the strategy is formulated and implemented in practice. It is very complex, and many planned strategies fail. Dr Andrew MacLennan: *"few organizations have discovered how to make strategy work reliably – the failure rate of planned strategies remains remarkably high."*

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791195131226.webp|600]]

> [!example] 🎓 Lecture slide – not in the book PDF (newer-edition figure)
> **What it shows:** a large green oval labelled **Context**, *"the factors, both internal (organisational structure, culture, etc.) and external (domestic and international market conditions) that affect operations strategy process and content"*. Inside it are two boxes linked by a **two-way arrow**:
> - **Process** – *how operations strategy decisions are made*;
> - **Content** – *decisions that form the operations strategy*.
> Arrows from the context point **into** both boxes.
> **How to explain it:** context surrounds and conditions everything. The process (how we decide) and the content (what we decide) influence each other, and in reality they overlap.

## 2.3 Top-down: does operations strategy reflect business strategy?

There are three levels: **corporate** strategy (which businesses and where) → **business** strategy (how each business competes) → **functional** strategies (operations, marketing, finance…).

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791376701008.webp]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 2.4 – Correspondence and coherence · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=76|p.76]]
> Two requirements, shown as two kinds of arrow:
> - **Correspondence (vertical arrows):** each level must match the level above. Business strategy ↔ operations strategy, and operations strategy ↔ individual operations decisions (innovation, structure and scope, process design, supply chain, capacity…).
> - **Coherence (horizontal arrows):** strategies at the *same* level must not contradict each other. Across functions (HR, marketing, operations, finance, IT) and within operations strategy.

### Business model vs operating model

> [!PDF|red] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=75|p.75]]
> Put simply, a 'business model' is the plan that is implemented by a company to generate revenue and make a profit (or fulfil its social objectives if a not-for-profit enterprise). It includes the various parts and organisational functions of the business, as well as the revenues it generates and the expenses it incurs. In other words, what a company does and how it makes money from doing it.

Common elements of a business model: (1) value proposition, (2) target customer segments, (3) communication and distribution channels, (4) customer relationships, (5) core capabilities, (6) configuration of activities, (7) partners, (8) revenue streams, (9) cost structure.

An **operating model** describes *how* the business model will be achieved. It is like an operations strategy applied to **all** functions. Typical elements: KPIs (with relative importance of objectives), core financial structure, accountabilities, organisational structure (often as capability areas), systems and technologies, processes and responsibilities, key knowledge. A **target operating model (TOM)** is how the organisation *should* operate in the future, so it implies change.

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791377142909.webp]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 2.5 – Business model and operating model overlap · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=78|p.78]]
> - The **upper** part of the oval (business strategy → functional strategies) is the **business model**. It *sets the overall purpose and objectives* for the operating model (left arrow).
> - The **lower** part (operational marketing, operations, finance, technology) is the **operating model**. It *determines how the business model will be achieved* (right arrow).
> - The blue spikes are the questions the operating model must answer: **people skills and capabilities? performance measures? systems and technology? resources? business processes? responsibilities?**
> 🎓 Vinelli's note: *"all operations are done to achieve the planned goal."*

## 2.4 Outside-in: market requirements

The five performance objectives (from Ch 1) are the operations translation of what the market wants.

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791377607658.webp]]

> [!info] 📖 Table 2.2 – Same objectives, different meaning · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=79|p.79]]
> Compares an **insurance company** (service) with a **steel plant** (manufacturing):
> - *Quality:* friendliness and accuracy of information **vs** % of products conforming to specification.
> - *Speed:* call-centre response and fast claims **vs** lead time from enquiry to quotation and from order to delivery.
> - *Dependability:* reliability of the promised date **vs** % of deliveries on time and in full.
> - *Flexibility:* customising insurance terms **vs** range of sizes and coatings, and changing quantity and timing of an order.
> - *Cost:* premium charged and 'no-claims' deals **vs** price of products and payment terms.

> [!PDF|note] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=80|p.80]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: The interpretation of the five performance objectives will differ between different operations.

### Order-winners and qualifiers

> [!PDF|red] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=81|p.81]]
> - Order-winners – are those things that directly and significantly contribute to winning business. They are regarded by customers as key reasons for purchasing the product or service. Raising performance in an order-winner will either result in more business or improve the chances of gaining more business.
> - Qualifiers – […] are those aspects of competitiveness where the operation's performance has to be above a particular level just to be considered by the customer. […] But any further improvement above the qualifying level is unlikely to gain the company much competitive benefit.

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791377748814.webp]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 2.6 – Order-winners vs qualifiers · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=80|p.80]]
> - **X:** achieved performance. **Y:** competitive benefit (negative, neutral, positive).
> - **Order-winner (blue straight line):** every improvement brings more benefit, steadily.
> - **Qualifier (red S-curve):** below the *qualifying level* it is very **negative** (you are excluded). Once you pass the level, the curve flattens near neutral, so being even better adds almost nothing.
> - **How to explain it:** qualifiers are "the givens of doing business"; order-winners are why customers choose you.
> 🎓 Vinelli: **"Yesterday's order-winner is today's qualifier."** Customers become happier, but the bar is raised for every company (e.g. car reliability).

> [!important] OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE
> Operations strategy should reflect the requirements of the business's markets. ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=80|p.80]]) · Different customer needs imply different priorities of performance objectives. ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=81|p.81]])

### The product/service life cycle and performance objectives

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791379072444.webp]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 2.7 – Life cycle effects on objectives · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=83|p.83]]
> The S-shaped sales curve is split into four stages. Read each column top to bottom:
>
> | | Introduction | Growth | Maturity | Decline |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Customers | Innovators | Early adopters | Bulk of market | Laggards |
> | Competitors | Few/none | Increasing | Stable | Declining |
> | Variety | High customisation | Increasingly standardised | Dominant types | Commodity |
> | Order-winners | Product novelty/performance | Availability of quality products | Low price, dependable supply | Low price |
> | Qualifiers | Quality, range | Price, range | Range, quality | Dependable supply |
> | **Dominant objectives** | **Flexibility, quality** | **Speed, dependability, quality** | **Cost, dependability** | **Cost** |
>
> **Logic:** uncertainty at the start calls for flexibility; keeping up with rapid growth calls for speed and dependability; once the market is mature and competing on price, cost dominates.

## 2.5 Bottom-up and inside-out (brief, for completeness)

- **Bottom-up:** strategy should **learn from operational experience**: capture the learning from routine activities and turn it into strategically valuable knowledge. Trends often show up first at the day-to-day level. Book example: Micraytech's **modular design** emerged from engineers' experience, not from a board decision ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=85|p.85]]).
- **Inside-out:** build **operations capabilities** from tangible *and intangible* resources (supplier relationships, reputation, know-how, teamwork). Strategic resources are scarce, imperfectly mobile, imperfectly imitable and imperfectly substitutable; test them with **VRIO** (Valuable, Rare, Inimitable, Organised to exploit) ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=87|p.87–88]]).

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=86|p.86]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: The long-term objective of operations strategy is to build operations-based capabilities.

## 2.6 Diagnostic question: are the four perspectives reconciled?

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791380476922.webp]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 2.9 – The four perspectives applied to Micraytech · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=90|p.90]]
> A worked example of Figure 2.3 for a metrology (measuring-equipment) company:
> - **Top-down:** the group builds capability in high-tech products, so Micraytech grows through *systems-based innovation*.
> - **Outside-in:** the growth market is *integrated metrology systems*. That requires software, data exchange and client-liaison skills, while keeping the position in individual devices.
> - **Bottom-up:** customers are confused by continual innovation and costs rise, so the company experiments with *modular design*.
> - **Inside-out:** not all modules can be integrated, so it must buy in technical capabilities and develop suppliers.
> - **Centre (the reconciled strategy):** *modular-based hi-tech systems that give customised flexibility and innovation at relatively low cost, through close relationships with clients and suppliers.*

### The operations strategy matrix

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791380599088.webp]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 2.10 – Operations strategy matrix (parcel courier) · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=91|p.91]]
> - **Rows = performance objectives** (market requirements, outside-in), ranked with stars: quality of service ★★★, delivery dependability ★★★, innovation ★★, cost ★★, range ★.
> - **Columns = strategic decision areas** (how resources are used, inside-out): *design*, *delivery*, *development*.
> - **Each filled cell** is a key intersection, the decision that delivers that objective. Quality is delivered by *track-and-trace technology* and a *knowledge management system*; dependability by *depot location*, *demand–capacity management* and a *vehicle maintenance programme*; cost by *process standardisation*, *route scheduling* and *location*.
> - Empty cells are not "no relationship". They are simply not critical for this strategy.
> **Use:** to check that market requirements and capabilities fit together at a given moment. Its limit is that it is **static**.

### The 'line of fit' model (reconciliation over time)

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791380753325.webp]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 2.11 – The line of fit · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=91|p.91]]
> - **Y = market requirements** (outside-in). **X = operations capabilities** (inside-out). The diagonal is the **line of fit**, where the two are balanced.
> - **A → D:** the ideal path, moving from fit at a low level (A) to fit at a high level (D, more profitable).
> - **B (below the line):** capabilities improved but not exploited in the market, so potential is **wasted**.
> - **C (above the line):** the company promises the market more than operations can deliver, so it **risks its reputation or brand**. The book notes C is possibly *more damaging* than B.
> - **How to explain it:** progress is rarely smooth. Below the line you under-exploit; above the line you over-promise.

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=91|p.91]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: An operation positioned on the 'line of fit' has operations capabilities that match its market requirements.

### Trade-offs and the 'efficient frontier'

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791380934203.webp]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 2.12 – The efficient frontier · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=94|p.94]]
> - **Axes:** variety (Y) vs cost efficiency (X), two objectives that usually trade off (more variety means more complexity and higher cost).
> - **(a)** A, B, C and D lie on the convex **efficient frontier**: each chose a different balance, but none is dominated. **X is inside the frontier**, so it is dominated (A offers more variety at the same cost efficiency; C offers the same variety with better cost efficiency). X should first **improve its effectiveness** before repositioning.
> - **(b)** Operation B adopts a new practice (Micraytech's **modular design**) and reaches **B1**, beyond the old frontier. It **pushes out the frontier**, improving variety *and* cost at the same time.
> - **Key distinction for the oral:** *repositioning along* the frontier (choosing a different trade-off) vs *pushing out* the frontier (overcoming the trade-off through better operations practice).

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=94|p.94]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: Operations that lie on the 'efficient frontier' have performance levels that dominate those that do not.
