# Chapter 4 – Operations scope and structure

> [!abstract] In one breath (for the oral)
> An operation sits inside a **supply network** (upstream suppliers, downstream customers, two-way flow of items and information). Its **scope** (what it does itself) is set by **vertical integration** and **outsourcing**. Its **structure** (what the network looks like) is set by **configuration**, **long-term capacity** and **location**. All these decisions rely on **demand forecasts** → [[Ch04S - Forecasting-claude|Supplement: Forecasting]].

[[Ch02 - Operations strategy-claude|← Ch 2]]

---

## Executive summary of the book (the 6 diagnostic questions)
[[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=142|p.142–143]]
1. **Place in the supply network:** structure = the network's shape and form; scope = what the operation does itself vs asks suppliers to do. Understanding both helps with competitiveness, significant links and long-term issues.
2. **Vertical integration:** defined by **direction, extent and balance**; a matter of balancing pros and cons.
3. **In-house or outsource:** outsourcing = *"an arrangement in which one company provides services for another company that could also be, or usually have been, provided in-house"*. It differs from vertical integration mainly in **scale and direction**. Consider performance objectives, **strategic importance**, **relative performance** and the **risk of supply disruption**.
4. **Configuration:** trends include fewer suppliers, disintermediation, co-opetition, business ecosystems.
5. **Capacity:** optimum capacity per site, balance between sites, timing of changes; economies and diseconomies of scale.
6. **Location:** move only if benefits > costs and disruption. Usually prompted by changes in **demand or supply**. Factors: labour, land, utilities, image, customer convenience, the site itself.

## 4.1 Does the operation understand its place in its supply network?

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=144|p.144]]
> 'A supply network is an interconnection of organizations that relate to each other through upstream and downstream linkages between the different processes and activities that produce value in the form of products and services to the ultimate consumer.

![[Pasted image 20261002095846.png]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 4.2 – Supply network terminology · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=144|p.144]]
> - **Centre:** the **focal operation** (the one we analyse), next to **competitors/collaborators** at the same level.
> - **Left = upstream (supply side):** *first-tier suppliers* supply the focal operation directly; *second-tier suppliers* supply the first tier, and so on.
> - **Right = downstream (demand side):** *first-tier customers* buy directly; *second-tier customers* are the customers' customers, up to the end consumer.
> - **How to explain it:** "Everything to the left feeds me, everything to the right I feed. 'Tier' counts the steps away from me."

### Two-way flow

> [!PDF|red] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=145|p.145]]
> […] along with the forward flow of transformed resources (materials, information and customers) in the network, each customer–supplier linkage will feed back orders and information. […] So flow is a two-way process with items flowing one way and information flowing the other.

- **Physical flow** (downstream): materials, parts, products, sometimes people.
- **Information flow** (upstream): orders, demand information, feedback.

Further terminology ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=144|p.144]]): some second-tier suppliers may supply the operation **directly**, skipping a link (and likewise on the customer side). Suppliers and customers in direct contact with the operation form its **immediate supply network**.

**Service supply networks** ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=145|p.145]]): services have networks too. Financial services can be seen as a flow of **information**. A **shopping mall** has security, cleaning and maintenance suppliers (who in turn use recruitment agencies and consultants), and its first-tier customers are the **retailers** leasing space, who serve shoppers. What really flows is **"operations performance"** (quality, speed, dependability, flexibility, cost): *all supply networks exist to facilitate the flow of operations performance*. This is a unifying concept.

### Why consider the *whole* network (not just immediate partners)
1. **It helps understand competitiveness.** Immediate customers and suppliers are the main concern, but you sometimes need to look further to see *why* they behave as they do. To understand end customers you either rely on all the intermediaries or look beyond them yourself. Relying only on them means trusting their judgement on things central to you.
2. **It helps identify significant links.** Not every operation has the same influence on what the end customer values. Book example, **domestic plumbing parts**: the important end customers are the installers; the **stock holders** that supply them are the **key players** (they must have everything in stock and deliver fast); suppliers to stock holders contribute through short lead times but **mainly through dependable delivery**.
3. **It helps focus on long-term issues.** Monitor technology and market changes. Example: **high-street music stores displaced by streaming and downloads**.

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=146|p.146]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: A supply network perspective helps to make sense of competitive, relationship and longer-term operations issues.

### Scope and structure

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=147|p.147]]
> The scope of an operation's activities within the network is determined by two decisions: 1. The extent and nature of the operation's vertical integration. 2. The nature and degree of outsourcing it engages in. The structure of an operation's supply network is determined by three sets of decisions: 1. How the network should be configured. 2. The long-term capacity decision – what physical capacity each part of the network should have. 3. The location decision – where each part of the network should be located. Note, however, that all of these decisions rely on forecasts of future demand […]

| | Decisions |
|---|---|
| **SCOPE** (what *we* do) | vertical integration · outsourcing |
| **STRUCTURE** (what the *network* looks like) | configuration · capacity · location |

![[Pasted image 20261002104812.png]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 4.3 – Shopping mall: scope vs structure · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=147|p.147]]
> The mall is unhappy with its security supplier and has three options:
> - **Option 1 – replace the security supplier:** still three suppliers doing the same jobs. **Structure the same, scope the same.**
> - **Option 2 – the cleaning supplier also provides security:** now two suppliers (cleaning+security and maintenance). **Structure changed, scope the same** (the mall does nothing new).
> - **Option 3 – the mall does security itself** (its own payroll): two suppliers left **and** a new activity in-house. **Structure changed, scope changed.**
> **Takeaway:** changing *who* supplies changes structure; changing *what you do yourself* changes scope.

## 4.2 How vertically integrated should the operation's network be?

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=148|p.148]]
> The scope of an operation's supply network determines to what extent an operation does things itself and the extent to which it will rely on other operations to do things for it. This is often referred to as 'vertical integration' when it is ownership of whole operations that are being decided, or 'outsourcing' when individual activities are being considered.

**Vertical integration** = the extent to which an organisation **owns** the network of which it is a part; it usually means assessing whether to acquire suppliers or customers. In the wind turbine industry, **OEMs** assemble the **nacelles** (which house generator and gearbox); towers and blades are built to OEM specs in-house or outside; installing means assembling on site, erecting and connecting to the grid.

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=149|p.149]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: Vertical integration is the extent to which an organisation owns the network of which it is a part. It is defined by the direction, extent and balance of integration.

A vertical integration strategy is defined by three dimensions:
- **Direction:** **backward/upstream** (buy suppliers, for cost advantage or to stop competitors controlling key supply) or **forward/downstream** (buy customers, to get closer to the market, contact end customers directly and possibly **sell complementary products and services**).
- **Extent of process span:** how far from the original stage the company goes. Some deliberately integrate very little.
- **Balance among stages:** how much of each stage's capacity is devoted to supplying the next stage. A *totally balanced* network is one where each stage produces only for the next and fully satisfies it.

![[Pasted image 20261002112151.png]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 4.4 – Three wind-turbine companies with different integration positions · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=148|p.148]]
> The chain is *design → manufacture nacelle parts → assemble nacelle → (manufacture tower/blades) → install → operate*. The **green boxes** are the stages owned by each company:
> - **Company A** owns design, nacelle parts and assembly: a **manufacturer**, integrated *upstream* but not downstream.
> - **Company B** owns design, assembly and installation: integrated *downstream* into installing, but buys parts and towers.
> - **Company C** owns almost the whole chain up to *operate*: **highly vertically integrated** (it even runs the wind farms).
> **Takeaway:** same industry, three different **process spans** and directions of integration.

### Advantages vs disadvantages of vertical integration
Extensive vertical integration is **less popular than it once was**, but very few companies are anywhere close to "virtual" (no integration at all). Book examples to quote ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=149|p.149–151]]):
- **Secure supply:** **oil companies** that sell gasoline also extract it to secure long-term supply. Sometimes the supply market lacks capacity, so firms must supply themselves.
- **Cost:** beware comparing *marginal direct cost* with the supplier's price; include **start-up and learning costs**. A clear technical case: **aluminium kitchen foil**, where rolling and slitting in-house saves loading, unloading and transport. **Transaction costs** = expenses other than price (searching and selecting suppliers, monitoring, negotiating contracts).
- **Quality / uniqueness:** protect the "secret ingredient" in fizzy drinks, or a complex technological process.
- **Understanding:** **McDonald's** franchises most restaurants but owns some outlets, otherwise "how else could it understand its retail operations?".
- **Loss of focus** is "arguably the most powerful" argument against.

| ✅ Advantages | ❌ Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **Secure, dependable access** to supply or to customers; control over market positioning (e.g. **Apple** designs hardware and some software in-house) | **Internal monopoly**: an internal supplier lacks competitive pressure to improve |
| **Reduced transaction costs** (but if purchase price + transaction cost < internal cost, integration is not justified) | **No economies of scale**: specialist suppliers serve many customers and are cheaper |
| **Quality / technological advantage**: keeps product knowledge away from competitors | **Loss of flexibility**: high fixed costs, so a drop in volume pushes you near or below break-even |
| **Understanding** of other activities in the network | **Cut off from innovation**: sunk investments make you slow to adopt new technology |
| | **Loss of focus** on the few core activities |

## 4.3 How do operations decide what to do in-house and what to outsource?

- **Vertical integration vs outsourcing:** the difference is **scale**. Vertical integration usually applies to **whole operations** (buying a supplier to deny it to a competitor; selling your servicing business to a specialist). Outsourcing applies to **smaller sets of activities** previously done in-house (sending quality tests to a specialist lab; handing your call centre to a larger call-centre company) ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=151|p.151]]).
- It is also called the **"do-or-buy" decision**. A larger share of direct activities, and many **indirect and administrative processes**, are now bought in: **business process outsourcing (BPO)** (routine back-office work in financial services; HR from payroll to training). The process may stay physically where it was, but the provider manages staff and technology. The motive is mainly **cost**, sometimes also **quality and flexibility**.

### Making the outsourcing decision

> [!PDF|note] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=151|p.151]]
> […] the question itself is relatively simple, even if the decision is not: 'Is it in-house or outsourced supply in a particular set of circumstances that gives the company the appropriate performance objectives that it requires to compete more effectively in its markets?'

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=151|p.151]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: Assessing the advisability of outsourcing should include how it impacts on relevant performance objectives.

![[Summary SOSCM - Macchion-1791795777555.webp]]

> [!info] 📖 Table 4.1 – In-house vs outsourced, by performance objective · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=152|p.152]]
> | Objective | "Do it yourself" in-house | "Buy it in" outsourced |
> |---|---|---|
> | Quality | Problems easier to trace and fix, but risk of complacency | Supplier may have specialised knowledge and market pressure, but communication is harder |
> | Speed | Synchronised schedules, but internal customers may get low priority | Speed can be written into the contract, but transport delays are possible |
> | Dependability | Easier communication | Late-delivery penalties, but organisational barriers |
> | Flexibility | Close to the real needs of the business, but limited by scale | Bigger suppliers can respond better, but balance conflicting customers |
> | Cost | No supplier margin to pay, but low volumes and no economies of scale | **Main reason to outsource**: supplier's economies of scale, but coordination costs |
> 🎓 Your class additions: in-house keeps the **know-how** and gives **control and coordination** and freedom over the line and order sequence; outsourcing helps when you **cannot afford the machinery**, when the **partner knows more**, and when **demand is highly variable** (you choose how much to order).

![[Summary SOSCM - Macchion-1791797223921.webp]]

> [!info] 📖 Case example – Compass and Vodafone: two ends of the outsourcing phenomenon · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=152|p.152–153]]
> - **Compass Group** is the world's biggest outsourced **catering** provider (factories, offices, schools, oil rigs, the military, sporting events; clients include Google, Microsoft, Nike, HSBC, Intel). Catering is *not core* for most firms and Compass has scale and expertise, so outsourcing to it makes sense.
> - **Vodafone** *brought back* (**reshored**) 2,000 call-centre jobs from overseas (South Africa) to the UK. The aim was to **enhance the quality** of its UK consumer customer service and align it with its UK-based business call centres. Customer service is close to the customer experience, so it is a strategic activity. The case also notes that **legislation** can matter: Italy introduced a law giving callers the option to speak to a call-centre worker in Italy rather than overseas.
> **Takeaway:** the same decision (outsource or not) has opposite answers depending on how **strategic** the activity is and on its effect on customers.

### Incorporating strategic factors

> [!PDF|note] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=153|p.153]]
> […] if an activity has long-term strategic importance to a company, it is unlikely to outsource it. […] Nor would a company usually outsource an activity for which it had specialised skills or knowledge.

![[Summary SOSCM - Macchion-1791797290965.webp]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 4.5 – Decision logic: outsource or not? · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=153|p.153]]
> A chain of four questions. **Any "Yes" means explore keeping the activity in-house:**
> 1. Is the activity of **strategic importance**?
> 2. Does the company have **specialised knowledge**?
> 3. Is the company's operations performance **superior**?
> 4. Is a significant operations performance **improvement likely**?
> Only if all four answers are **"No"** should you **explore outsourcing this activity**.

> [!danger] OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE
> Assessing the advisability of outsourcing should include consideration of the strategic importance of the activity and the relative operations performance.

Book examples of strategic factors ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=153|p.153]]): a **retailer** keeps **website development** in-house, even if specialists are cheaper, because it plans to move into web retailing. A **laser-printer maker** keeps the production of **laser drives** because that specialised knowledge enables future innovation; *"it would be foolish to 'give away' such capability"*.

### Outsourcing vs offshoring, globalisation and reshoring
[[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=154|p.154]]
- **Outsourcing** = buying in instead of doing in-house. **Offshoring** = obtaining products or services from operations **outside one's own country**. You can do both. The motives are similar (offshoring to lower-cost regions; outsourcing to suppliers with more expertise or scale).
- **Globalisation:** driven by labour-cost differences, cheap telecommunications, trade deals and lower transport costs (Apple designs in California and assembles in Asia; a French aerospace firm directs its Brazilian suppliers). Bill Clinton called it *"the economic equivalent of a force of nature"*.
- **Is the boom over? Reshoring:** protectionism, rising wages in previously less-developed countries, and the benefits of nearby suppliers (lower transport and inventory costs, less pollution, less **reputational risk**, more flexibility). **Zara** makes **steady-selling** items in low-cost Asian factories but makes **less predictable** garments **close to its markets** to respond to fashion. **Automation** may enable **"radical insourcing"**.
- **Risk and reshoring:** Vodafone reshored for perceived service. **COVID-19** made firms weigh the risk of depending on offshore supply: demand for "non-essential" goods dried up while medical supplies became scarce *everywhere at once*. No alternative arrangement would have fully saved them, but **shorter chains and in-house supply give a sense of control**, so some firms seriously considered bringing supply closer or back in-house.

## 4.4 What configuration should a supply network have?

**Configuring** = determining the overall pattern, shape and arrangement of the network. Even without owning other operations, a firm can **reconfigure** to change the nature of relationships, sometimes by merging responsibilities ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=155|p.155]]).
- **Reducing the number of direct suppliers** is the most common reconfiguration: dealing with hundreds of suppliers is costly and prevents **close relationships**.
- **Disintermediation:** cutting out the middlemen, i.e. bypassing customers or suppliers to reach customers' customers or suppliers' suppliers directly. Example: airlines and hotels selling directly online instead of through travel agents ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=155|p.155]]).
- **Co-opetition:** a business is surrounded by **suppliers, customers, competitors and complementors**. Complementors make your product *more* valuable (because customers can also have theirs); competitors make it *less* valuable. The same firm can be both ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=155|p.155]]).
  - Example: **adjacent restaurants** compete for the customer standing outside, but they are also **complementors**: would the customer come to that part of town if there were only one? Restaurants, theatres, galleries and tourist attractions **cluster** to enlarge the joint market. Distinguish **cooperating to grow the market** from **competing for a share** of it.
  - Customers and suppliers should have **"symmetric" roles**: destroying value in a supplier to create it for a customer doesn't increase the network's value, so pressuring suppliers doesn't necessarily add value. Everyone can be friend and enemy at different times: **co-opetition** ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=155|p.155–156]]).
- **Business ecosystem:** *"An economic community supported by a foundation of interacting organizations and individuals – the organisms of the business world. […] Over time, they coevolve their capabilities and roles, and tend to align themselves with the directions set by one or more central companies."* Unlike a supply network, it also includes businesses with **no direct supply relationship** that exist *because of* the network and add to the value proposition. Typical of tech industries: **app developers** for an operating-system platform aren't "suppliers", but the relationship is mutually beneficial, it increases the core product's value, and it creates **barriers to entry**, because a competitor must fight the *whole ecosystem* ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=156|p.156]]).

### Describing supply networks: dyads and triads
[[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=156|p.156–157]] · Figure 4.6
- **Dyad** = the interaction between **two** operations (supplier–focal operation, focal operation–customer). For years most research was dyadic.
- **Triad** = buyer, supplier **and** customer. Many argue triads are the basic element, especially in **services**, where buyers outsource service delivery to providers who deal **directly with the buyer's customers**. Book examples: an **airline** contracting a **baggage-handling agent** to serve passengers; a company outsourcing **IT** (purchaser, IT provider, employees using IT); an **electric-motor maker supplying a washing-machine maker**, whose products reach the **retailer**.
- **Why triads matter strategically** (5 points): (1) **dependence** on the supplier's performance, which is how the buyer is judged; (2) **less control**, because the service goes straight from provider to customer and can't be intervened in; (3) **power may shift** over time to the provider; (4) the buyer finds it hard to know what happens day to day, and the supplier may not report honestly; (5) **lost learning**: e.g. an equipment maker outsourcing maintenance loses knowledge of how customers use the equipment. *It outsourced the cost and trouble, but also the benefits and learning.*

### Structural complexity: from single-channel to omnichannel
A dyadic chain is simpler than a triad, and many interlinked triads are more complex still, especially when **customers choose how and when to interact** with the network ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=158|p.158–159]]). Single-channel: online and physical kept separate. Multi-channel: channels treated as **independent**, each aligned to a target segment. Cross-channel: first **integration** of stores with web. Omnichannel: seen as one of the most important retail developments since online services.

![[Summary SOSCM - Macchion-1791799196599.webp]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 4.7 – Single-, multi-, cross- and omnichannel retail · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=158|p.158]]
> Moving right means **more options for customers** (Y) and **more operations complexity** (X):
> - **Single-channel:** customer ↔ store only.
> - **Multi-channel:** several channels (store, phone, app, web, social), but each is **separate**, aimed at a different segment.
> - **Cross-channel:** channels start to **connect** (a curved link through the customer).
> - **Omnichannel:** **all channels fully integrated** with each other and around the customer: browse on social media, order on the app, pay by phone, return in the store.
> This needs coordination between marketing, retail operations, distribution and IT.

> [!info] 📖 Case example – Amazon and Bonobos move to include bricks and mortar · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=159|p.159]]
> Online pure players opened physical stores. **Amazon** started with bookstores, acquired **Whole Foods Market** and opened cashier-less **Amazon Go**. **Bonobos** (online menswear, owned by Walmart) opened stores that **hold no stock**: customers try items on and the order is shipped from the online network. In other words, the store separates the *experience* from the *fulfilment*. This shows the network configuration moving towards omnichannel.

## 4.4 How much capacity should each part of the network have?

### The optimum capacity level

> [!PDF|note] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=160|p.160]]
> […] Low occupancy, because of few customers, will result in a high cost per customer served because the fixed costs of the operation are being shared between few customers. […] operating at very high levels of capacity utilisation […] can mean longer customer waiting times and reduced customer service. […] This usually means that average costs start to increase after a point that will often be lower than the theoretical capacity of the operation.

![[Summary SOSCM - Macchion-1791888923909.webp]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 4.8 – Unit-cost curves for truck service centres · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=160|p.160]]
> - Each **blue U-curve** is one centre size (5, 10, 15 bays). Cost per bay falls as occupancy rises (fixed costs are spread), then rises again near full capacity (overtime, no time for maintenance, breakdowns).
> - The **red envelope** is the *economy-of-scale curve* for capacity size. Up to about **10 bays** bigger is cheaper (**economies of scale**). Beyond that, costs rise again (**diseconomies of scale**: complexity, coordination).
> - **Takeaway:** there is an optimum *size* of capacity, and for each size an optimum *utilisation* that is below 100%.

> [!important] OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE
> All types of operations exhibit economy-of-scale effects where operating costs reduce as the scale of capacity increases.

**Why economies of scale** ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=160|p.160]]): fixed costs don't grow proportionally (a 10-bay centre has *less than twice* the fixed costs of a 5-bay one), and neither does the **capital cost** of building. **Why diseconomies of scale** ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=161|p.161]]): (1) **complexity costs**, since communication and coordination effort grows faster than capacity; (2) a large unit is more likely to be **underutilised**, because demand at a fixed location is limited. For physical goods the equivalent is **transport costs** (one plant in Denmark serving all of Europe). Capacity in this chapter is the **long-term** view; medium and short term are in Ch 8.

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=161|p.161]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: Diseconomies of scale increase operating costs above a certain level of capacity, resulting in a minimum cost-level of capacity.

**Being small may have advantages** (a research study, [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=161|p.161]]):
1. **Locating near "hot spots"** to tap local knowledge networks (large firms centralise R&D and lose touch).
2. **Responding rapidly to regional needs** with more, smaller units close to local markets.
3. **Human resource development:** more local autonomy and chances of "taking charge".
4. **Exploring radically new technologies** like an entrepreneurial rival (large centres are more bureaucratic).

### The timing of capacity change

> [!PDF|note] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=161|p.161]]
> 1. Capacity is introduced to generally lead demand […] 2. Capacity is introduced to generally lag demand […] 3. Capacity is introduced to sometimes lead and sometimes lag demand, but inventory built up during the 'lead' times is used to help meet demand during the 'lag' times. This is called 'smoothing with inventory'

![[Summary SOSCM - Macchion-1791889263354.webp]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 4.9 – Capacity leading, lagging and smoothing · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=162|p.162]] (with your handwritten notes)
> - The **black S-curve** is forecast demand. Capacity can only be added in **steps** (a new machine or plant).
> - **(a) Red dashed steps above the curve = capacity leads demand:** there is always enough capacity (a capacity cushion), so revenue is maximised, but utilisation is low and costs are high.
> - **(a) Blue dashed steps below the curve = capacity lags demand:** the operation is always full, so unit cost is minimal, but some demand is lost and customers are dissatisfied. Your notes mark the **red circle** as the *point at which demand matches capacity*, and the red/blue lines as "two different situations".
> - **(b) Smoothing with inventory:** the steps cross the curve. When capacity is above demand you **build inventory**, and you use it when capacity is below demand. Your note: *"something with inventories means using the excess capacity in one period to produce inventory that supplies the under-capacity period."*

| Strategy | ✅ | ❌ |
|---|---|---|
| **Lead** | Always meets demand; cushion for pessimistic forecasts; start-up problems don't hit supply | Low utilisation, high cost; risk of permanent over-capacity; early capital spending |
| **Lag** | Full utilisation, minimum unit cost; limited over-capacity if forecasts are optimistic; capital spending delayed | Lost revenue, unhappy customers; can't exploit demand surges; start-up problems worsen under-supply |
| **Smooth with inventory** | All demand met; high utilisation | High **working capital** in stock; risk of deterioration or obsolescence |

*(Table 4.2, [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=162|p.162]])*

- The choice depends on how the company weighs these pros and cons: with **limited capital**, the **lag** strategy's delayed spending is attractive.
- **Smoothing with inventory works only for storable products**. A **hotel** can't satisfy this year's demand with rooms that were empty last year ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=161|p.161]]).

> [!important] OPERATIONS PRINCIPLES
> Capacity-leading strategies increase opportunities to meet demand. Capacity-lagging strategies increase capacity utilisation.
> Using inventories to overcome demand–capacity imbalance tends to increase working capital requirements.

### Break-even analysis of capacity expansion

![[Summary SOSCM - Macchion-1791890237997.webp]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 4.10 – Fixed-cost breaks · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=163|p.163]]
> - **Revenue** is the blue straight line from the origin. **Total costs** are the dotted line, which **jumps** at every *fixed-cost break* (each new increment of capacity is a lump of fixed cost).
> - Just after each jump, total costs can sit **above** revenue, so the operation is **temporarily unprofitable** until volume grows enough.
> - **Takeaway:** expanding capacity can turn a profitable operation into a loss-making one over some output ranges. You need enough volume to absorb each new block of fixed cost.

## 4.6 Where should operations be located?

Location defines the network's structure and affects how it works: costs **and** the ability to serve customers (so revenue). Poor location hurts the whole network (e.g. a **data centre** where suitably skilled staff won't live). Location decisions are **hard to undo**: moving is very expensive and risks inconveniencing customers. Some operations are where they are **for historical reasons**, but even "there because they're there" is an implicit decision not to move ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=164|p.164]]).

### Why relocate?
- **Changes in demand:** customers move (garment makers moved to Asia, so zip and thread suppliers followed) or volume grows. High-visibility operations, such as a dry-cleaner, often can't expand on site and must open a new location ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=164|p.164]]).
- **Changes in supply:** cost or availability of inputs changes. Mines relocate when minerals run out; software companies located in India for talented, relatively cheap staff ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=165|p.165]]).

> [!important] OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE
> An operation should only change its location if the benefits of moving outweigh the costs of operating in the new location plus the cost of the move itself. ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=165|p.165]])

### Evaluating locations: be systematic
The evaluation is complex (many options and criteria, rarely one dominant site) and highly **uncertain**, so (a) identify the alternative options, and (b) evaluate each against rational criteria.

**Identifying options** ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=166|p.166]]):
- Always evaluate **"do nothing"** (not relocating) as the **base case**.
- Globalisation increases the number of options and the uncertainty, so the decision can't be "optimised". Select a **limited number of sites with different attributes** (e.g. a distribution centre close to transport links, but in different regions, near cities or rural).
- When the supply of sites is small (high-street retail), the decision becomes a **sequence of "take or wait" decisions**.

The five typical criteria ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=166|p.166–167]]):

| Criterion | What it includes |
|---|---|
| **Capital requirements** | Cost of buying or leasing the site, shape, access, utilities |
| **Market factors** | Customer convenience and image (a hospital in the countryside is a bad idea), labour market and skills. Remote Scottish call-centre staff needed *assertiveness training* to cope with aggressive callers |
| **Cost factors** | (1) cost of producing (labour, plus *community factors*: taxes, political stability, incentives, language, amenities, labour relations…) and (2) transport cost of inputs and outputs |
| **Future flexibility** | Will the site still work in possible futures? Use *scenario planning* |
| **Risk factors** | Exchange rates, labour costs, security of staff and property |

> [!example] 📖 Case example – IKEA closes a seven-storey store (Coventry) · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=166|p.166]]
> The city-centre site was cramped, so the store was built over **seven floors** to reach the standard IKEA floor area. That raised operating costs and worsened the shopping experience, while customers were moving to retail parks and online. It lost money consistently and was closed. **Lesson:** the site's *size and shape* can matter as much as its geographic location.

### Critical commentary
[[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=167|p.167–168]]
- **Outsourcing** is the most controversial issue. **Trade unions** say outsourcers are cheaper only because they **cut salaries and/or working conditions**, and that flexibility comes from **less job security**. Even supporters admit problems: **staff resistance**; **"outsourcing a problem"** (shipping out a badly managed process instead of fixing it); costs may **rise initially** while both sides learn the arrangement.
- **Business ecosystem:** some see it as a **buzzword** indistinguishable from the supply network. Others say the "natural" metaphor makes business look "green" and suggests commercial relationships should be **free from government or societal interference**.

### Summary checklist (book)
[[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=168|p.168]]: Are 1st- and 2nd-tier suppliers' capabilities and customers' requirements understood across all performance objectives? Is there a view of how the network should develop? Fewer suppliers explored? Disintermediation threats? An approach to co-opetitors? Is vertical integration and outsourcing always under review, evaluated on all objectives and with rational criteria? Is the optimum economy of scale reviewed? Are capacity-timing strategies evaluated? Are fixed-cost breaks understood? Have demand/supply changes prompting relocation been considered? Are locations compared with each other and with "do nothing", on capital, market, cost, flexibility and risk?

## 📚 Case examples and worked examples (Chapter 4)

> [!example] Case – **Hollywood: changing scope and structure** · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=145|p.145–146]]
> **Facts:** F. Scott Fitzgerald (*The Last Tycoon*): *"not half a dozen men have ever been able to keep the whole equation of [making] pictures in their heads"*. The "equation" balances **artistic creativity and fashion awareness** with **efficiency and tight operations**. The classic studio **did everything itself**: it employed everyone from carpenters to stars (Cary Grant was as much an employee as his chauffeur) and mass-produced and physically distributed film rolls. **Today** studios deal almost only in **ideas**: they buy and sell concepts, arrange finance, cut marketing deals and manage a **virtual network** of self-employed stars and small technical specialists. Assembling these teams is now the key skill, and it is harder to control (even for streaming services).
> **Why:** a dramatic shift from **high vertical integration** to a **network/outsourced** structure. Scope narrowed and structure changed.

> [!example] Case – **Compass and Vodafone** (see 4.3 above for the facts) · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=152|p.152–153]]
> Extra details: Compass serves **5.5 billion meals a year** in **55,000 client locations** with **600,000 employees**, organised by **sector** (business and industry, healthcare and seniors, education, sports and leisure, defence/offshore/remote). An in-house canteen can't match its variety, expertise, scale or awareness of food trends; Compass can also add events, reception, cleaning and facilities management. Getting outsourced catering wrong **upsets staff**; getting outsourced customer service wrong **upsets customers**, which hits revenue. Outsourcing customer service offers lower cost (low-cost countries), a wider range, the ability to absorb peaks and the latest technology, but it is always risky.

> [!example] Case – **Amazon and Bonobos move to bricks and mortar** · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=159|p.159–160]]
> First, traditional retailers went online (those that didn't often suffered). Then online players opened stores: first "big ticket" items (people want to see a **sofa**), then portable goods. Amazon: bookstores → **Whole Foods Market** → **Amazon Go** (cashier-less, identification technology). **Bonobos** (Walmart): stores **hold no stock**, which **separates purchase from distribution**. Customers "get the feel" without the costs of a conventional store (no expensive storage space; staff not distracted by restocking), and staff help find the fit, then the order is placed online. *They sold better because they didn't stock the clothes.*

> [!example] Worked example – **De Vere Graphics** (fixed-cost breaks) · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=163|p.163–164]]
> Machine capacity = 100,000 prints a year; fixed cost **€200,000 per machine per year**; variable cost **€1 per unit**; price **€4 per unit**. Demand: year 1 = 100,000; year 2 = 220,000.
> - **Year 1:** 1 machine. Cost = 200,000 + 1 × 100,000 = **€300,000**. Revenue = **€400,000**. **Profit = €100,000**.
> - **Year 2:** 220,000 units need **3 machines**. Cost = 3 × 200,000 + 1 × 220,000 = **€820,000**. Revenue = 4 × 220,000 = **€880,000**. **Profit = €60,000**.
> **Message:** demand more than doubles but **profit falls**, because the third machine is a **fixed-cost break** used for only 20,000 units. This is Figure 4.10 in numbers.
> 💡 *Tip:* break-even per machine = 200,000 / (4 − 1) ≈ 66,667 units. The third machine runs only 20,000 units, below its break-even, so it loses (4−1) × 20,000 − 200,000 = −€140,000.

> [!example] Case – **Singapore's aerospace success** (location) · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=165|p.165]]
> Singapore has almost **no internal air transport**, but it has **SIA** and has attracted **Airbus, Rolls-Royce, Pratt & Whitney, Thales, Bombardier**. Reasons:
> 1. **Skills and infrastructure** for complex manufacturing (no. 1 in the WEF Global Competitiveness Report 2019).
> 2. **Trusted ethical framework** (no. 1 in Asia for **intellectual property protection**).
> 3. **Demand is in Asia** (the fastest-growing airlines are in China, Singapore, Indonesia, India and the Gulf).
> 4. **Government incentives** (tax). But the **"soft" factors** matter more: universities and colleges providing a sustainable **talent pipeline**, aerospace companies partnering schools (courses, attachments, on-the-job training), and SMEs building capabilities in parts manufacturing and **MRO** (maintenance, repair and overhaul).
> **Oral use:** location criteria, especially market factors (labour skills), cost and **community factors**, and risk (IP).

> [!example] Case – **IKEA closes a seven-storey store** (Coventry) · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=166|p.166]] (details in 4.6 above)
> Closed after **more than 10 years**, while IKEA opened a big store in Hammersmith at almost the same time, so the problem wasn't big stores in general. Thousands queued at opening (police had to control crowds), yet the store **lost money consistently**; it couldn't be made more efficient or repurposed.

> [!example] End-of-chapter case study – **Aarens Electronic (AE)** · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=169|p.169–170]]
> **Context:** a precision coating and laminating firm near **Rotterdam** (COO **Francine Jansen**). Key customer **Phanchem**, supplied with **dry photoresist imaging films** for microchips. The new plant uses **"ultra-clean"** technology to win most or all of Phanchem's business.
> **Three capacity options:**
> 1. Expand within an existing site: **12–13 MSM/yr**, **€19m**.
> 2. New facility alongside the existing plant: **15 MSM/yr**, **€22m**, with room for expansion.
> 3. Totally new site: **~25 MSM/yr**, **≥ €30m**.
> The team favoured 2, but the board chose **3**: highest risk, but it fits the group's high-tech philosophy and could **change the industry's economics** (even with industry over-capacity, the low-cost, high-quality plant wouldn't be the idle one). It started at **32 MSM/yr**.
> **Process design:** **dedicated and inflexible** rather than flexible. Capital savings and **up to 25% lower operating costs**, easier **root-cause analysis**, total focus ("when the line is down we generate no revenue… we're either making it or we're not"). Risk: no market for it in a few years.
> **Result:** within 6 months, a **10-year contract for 100%** of Phanchem's needs.
> **Location of Phanchem:** product was shipped **~500 km** to Frankfurt (transit damage, delays). Options: (1) stay near Frankfurt (discarded: Phanchem wanted to sell that valuable site); (2) move to a site 30 km from Rotterdam (no advantage over being even closer); (3) a site across the road; (4) **co-locate in an extension attached to AE's plant** (chosen). The AE board objected ("we are manufacturers, not in the real estate business") and insisted on a door **lockable from both sides**. It has **never been locked**.
> **Book questions and hints:**
> 1. *Scope and structure decisions:* capacity size and timing (a big leading increment), location (new site, then co-location), dedicated process, single-customer relationship (close to partnership, Ch 7).
> 2. *Risks of total dedication:* dependence on one customer and one product, technological obsolescence, no fallback.
> 3. *Location options:* compare cost, transport damage, closeness of the relationship and flexibility. Co-location maximises integration and lowers both firms' costs.

> [!note] Book exercises ("Applying the principles", p.171), with the logic of each answer
> - *Music business as a supply network:* streaming and downloads **disintermediated** record shops, changed artists' revenues (fewer sales, more live shows and streaming) and moved power to the platforms. Use this for "long-term issues" and "disintermediation".
