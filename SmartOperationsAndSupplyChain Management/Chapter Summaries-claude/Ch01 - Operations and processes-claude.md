# Chapter 1 – Operations and processes

> [!abstract] In one breath (for the oral)
> Every organisation has **operations**, because every organisation produces some mix of products and services. Operations are made of **processes**, and every process is an **input → transformation → output** system. Processes are connected in **networks** at three levels (supply network, operation, process). What makes processes different is not the sector they are in but their **4 Vs** (volume, variety, variation, visibility). Operations contribute to the enterprise in five ways (**cost, revenue, risk, investment, capabilities**) and are judged on five objectives (**quality, speed, dependability, flexibility, cost**), all framed by the **triple bottom line**. Operations managers' work falls into the **4 Ds**: direct, design, deliver, develop.

## 1.1 Operations and process management

**Key definitions** (the same four that open the chapter and Vinelli's slides):

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Operations and process management** | Managing the resources and processes that produce products and services, for **internal and external** customers. |
| **Operations management** | How the *operations function* produces products and services for **external** customers. |
| **Process** | An arrangement of resources and activities that transforms inputs into outputs that satisfy (internal or external) customer needs. |
| **Operations** | Transform inputs into outputs. **All operations are composed of processes.** |

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=31|p.31]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: All operations are composed of processes.

### All enterprises have operations
- The operations function is the part of the organisation that produces products or services. Since **every** organisation produces something, every organisation has operations.
- It usually holds most of the company's assets and people, and it is how strategy becomes reality.
- 🎓 Class example: **H&M produces 0% of the clothes it sells**, yet it is full of operations managers: it designs, sources, distributes and sells. The *production* step is outsourced, the *operations* are not.

### Not all operations are the same

![[file-20260930184220550.png]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 1.2 – Different operations inside the same sector · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=28|p.28]]
> - **Rows = sectors** (financial services, furniture, hotels). **Columns = market position.**
> - Left column: value-for-money and high volume, competing on **cost** (bank call centre, mass kitchen units, budget hotel). Right column: upmarket and customised, competing on **specification/customisation** (investment advisor, craft furniture, luxury hotel).
> - **Takeaway:** two operations in the *same sector* can differ more than two operations in *different sectors*. Market position (volume, variety, how you compete) drives how operations must be managed, **not** the sector label.

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=27|p.27]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: The economic sector of an operation is less important in determining how it should be managed than its intrinsic characteristics.

### History: from production management to operations and supply chain management

![[file-20260930184700503.png]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 1.3 – How the scope of the subject grew · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=30|p.30]]
> Read the cube as **three expansions** starting from the small pink cube at the bottom-left, which stands for "production management of physical goods":
> 1. **Type of operation** (horizontal axis): from manufactured goods only → **all goods and services**.
> 2. **Type of process** (depth axis): from core production processes → **all processes in all functions** (marketing, finance, HR also run processes).
> 3. **Interaction between operations** (vertical axis): from one operation's internal processes → **networks and chains of operations**.
> The big pink cube at the top-right is today's **operations and supply chain management**.

The four "names" of the subject, in order:
1. **Production / manufacturing management.** Only factories.
2. **Operations management.** The ideas also apply to services, and the scope widens to related processes (purchasing, distribution, after-sales, product development).
3. **Operations and process management.** *Every* function is made of processes.
4. **Operations and supply chain management.** What happens outside the company's boundary (suppliers, customers) matters as much as what happens inside. The boundary itself is a decision: make or buy (→ Chapter 4).

## 1.2 The process perspective

> [!tip] Core idea
> An organisation is a **collection of interconnected processes**. Each function (marketing, finance, HR…) has its own technical knowledge, but also an **operations role**: it uses processes to produce *plans, policies, reports and services* for internal customers. So **every manager is, to some extent, an operations manager**.

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=33|p.33]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: All processes have inputs of transforming and transformed resources that they use to create products and services.

### The input–transformation–output model

![[file-20260930194217059.png]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 1.4 – Input → transformation → output · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=33|p.33]]
> - **Transformed resources** (top-left) are what gets *changed*: **materials, information, customers**.
> - **Transforming resources** (bottom-left) are what *does the changing*: **people and facilities** (buildings, equipment, technology). They are the "building blocks" of every process.
> - The output is **products and services** for customers.
> - Bank example: printing statements processes **materials**, account management processes **information**, branch advice processes **customers**.

### Front office and back office

![[Pasted image 20260930195328.png]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 1.5 – Front office vs back office · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=34|p.34]]
> When the transformed resource is the **customer**, split the operation in two:
> - **Front office** (pink, bottom): processes that act *on customers*, so **high customer visibility**.
> - **Back office** (blue, top): processes that support the front office (materials, information), so **low customer visibility**.
> People and facilities feed both. Why it matters: front-office processes must manage customer perception and waiting; back-office processes can be optimised for efficiency.

### The output: products and services
Outputs are products and/or services, and the line between them is often subtle (most offerings are a mix). Whatever the label, outputs must satisfy **customers**:
- **External customers** pay, so they are the organisation's reason for being.
- **Internal customers** matter just as much in the long run: poor internal service eventually becomes poor external service, higher costs, or both.

### Three levels of analysis

![[Pasted image 20261001133737.png]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 1.7 – Three levels of analysis · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=37|p.37]]
> Think of it as zooming in with a camera:
> 1. **Supply network** (top): flow *between operations*. You need to know each operation's role and how they relate. This is **strategic** analysis.
> 2. **Operation** (middle): flow *between processes* inside one operation.
> 3. **Process** (bottom): flow *between resources* (people and facilities) inside one process. This is **operational** analysis.
> The same input–transformation–output logic applies at every zoom level.

So operations and process management rests on two ideas: **(1)** the input–transformation–output model, and **(2)** processes connected in **networks**.

### End-to-end business processes

![[Pasted image 20261001134014.png]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 1.10 – Reorganising around end-to-end processes (Studio Division example) · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=46|p.46]]
> - **Top row:** the traditional, *functional* organisation (client relationship → set & props → engineering → production → planning → finance).
> - **Red arrows:** *end-to-end* processes that start from a customer need and end when it is fulfilled (pitch preparation, programme production, advertising contracts, technical support).
> - **Circles:** how much each function contributes to each end-to-end process (bigger circle = bigger contribution).
> - **Takeaway:** processes can be defined by **the customer need they fulfil** instead of by the function that owns them. This is the logic behind Business Process Re-engineering.

## 1.3 Operations performance at the level of the enterprise

### Triple bottom line (people, planet, profit)

> [!PDF|red] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=47|p.47]]
> One idea that tries to capture the idea of a broader approach to assessing an organisation's performance is the 'triple bottom line' (TBL, or 3BL), also known as 'people, planet and profit'. […] A sustainable business is one that creates an acceptable profit for its owners, but minimises the damage to the environment and enhances the existence of the people with whom it has contact. In other words, it balances economic, environmental and societal interests. This gives the organisation its 'license to operate' in society.

> [!important] OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE
> Operations should judge themselves on the triple bottom line principle of 'people, planet and profit'. ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=47|p.47]])

### The five ways operations contribute to the enterprise

| Contribution | How operations deliver it |
|---|---|
| **Cost** | Efficiency: the more productive the transformation, the lower the cost per unit. |
| **Revenue** | Quality, service and innovation keep customers and attract new ones. |
| **Risk** | Well-run operations fail less, and when they fail they recover faster (**resilience**). |
| **Investment** | Lower capital needed *per unit of output*, by raising effective capacity and using resources innovatively. |
| **Capabilities** | Each unit produced is a chance to learn. The knowledge accumulated is the basis of future innovation. |

> [!PDF|note] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=48|p.48]]
> It can build capabilities that will form the basis for future innovation by building a solid base of operations skills and knowledge within the business. Every time an operation produces a product or a service it has the opportunity to accumulate knowledge about how that product or service is best produced.

🎓 Class example on **capabilities**: why do luxury brands like **Bottega Veneta** keep production in Italy? Because that is where the craft capabilities are concentrated: the people who know how to make the product.

> [!important] OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE (to mention at the exam)
> All operations should be expected to contribute to their enterprise by controlling costs, increasing revenue, reducing risk, making investment more effective and growing long-term capabilities. ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=48|p.48]])

## 1.4 Operations performance at the level of processes: the five objectives

| Objective | Plain meaning | Class example |
|---|---|---|
| **Quality** | Doing things *right*: error-free, fit for purpose | |
| **Speed** | Doing things *fast*: short time from request to receipt | Amazon delivery volumes |
| **Dependability** | Doing things *on time*: keeping delivery promises | Zara: many collections a year, always on schedule |
| **Flexibility** | *Changing* what or how you do it: new products, mix, volume, delivery | |
| **Cost** | Doing things *cheaply*, so the price works for the market and still gives a return | |

> [!PDF|note] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=49|p.49]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: Operations and process performance at an operational level can be grouped together as quality, speed, dependability, flexibility and cost.

## 1.5 Diagnostic question: are processes managed to reflect their operating circumstances? The 4 Vs

Processes differ in **technical** content, and also in the **nature of their demand**, which the 4 Vs describe:
- **Volume:** how many.
- **Variety:** how many different products or services.
- **Variation in demand:** how much demand fluctuates and how predictable it is.
- **Visibility:** how much of the process the customer experiences.

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=50|p.50]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: The way in which processes need to be managed is influenced by volume, variety, variation in demand and visibility.

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791192524661.webp]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 1.11 – Implications of the 4 Vs · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=52|p.52]]
> Each arrow is one V. **Right side = the low-cost end; left side = the high-cost end.**
> - **Volume high →** repeatability, specialisation, systemisation, capital-intensive technology, **low unit cost**. (Low volume: each person does more of the job, so high unit cost.)
> - **Variety low →** well defined, routine, standardised, **low unit cost**. (High variety: flexible but complex, high cost.)
> - **Variation low →** stable, predictable, high utilisation, **low unit cost**. (High variation: capacity must keep changing, which needs anticipation and spare capacity.)
> - **Visibility low →** time lag between production and consumption, standardised, centralised, **low unit cost**. (High visibility: short waiting tolerance, customer perception matters, contact skills needed.)
> **One-line rule:** high volume and low variety, variation and visibility mean low cost.

🎓 Class examples: **Fincantieri** has very low volume (one ship at a time, highly customised), while **McDonald's** has high volume because it has low variety, which allows a standardised chain of work.

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=53|p.53]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: Operations and processes can (other things being equal) reduce their costs by increasing volume, reducing variety, reducing variation and reducing visibility.

### Charting processes with the 4 Vs: retail banking

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791193009933.webp]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 1.12 – 4 V profiles of retail banking processes · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=53|p.53]]
> Each coloured line is one process, crossing the four arrows:
> - **Personal banker/advisor** (dark blue, left): low volume, high variety, high variation, high visibility. This is the **most expensive** profile, so it is offered only to wealthy, high-profit clients.
> - **Bank branch** (purple) and **call centre** (green) sit in the middle.
> - **App-based transactions** (red, far right): high volume, low variety, low visibility, the **cheapest** profile.
> **Takeaway:** one company runs processes with very different 4 V positions, so each needs different management. New technology (apps, ATMs) has moved banking towards the low-cost end.

## 1.6 The operations manager's activities: the 4 Ds

| D | Activity |
|---|---|
| **Direct** | Set the overall operations strategy: strategic purpose, innovation, what to keep in-house vs outsource. |
| **Design** | Determine the physical form, shape and composition of operations and processes and their resources. |
| **Deliver** | Plan and control the flow from suppliers, through the operation, to customers. |
| **Develop** | Improve process capabilities and performance over time. |

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=54|p.54]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: Operations management activities can be grouped into four broad categories: directing the overall strategy of the operation; designing the operation's products, services and processes; planning and controlling delivery; and developing process performance.

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791193380459.webp|700]]

> [!example] 🎓 Lecture photo – Figure 1.14 of the newer edition (not in the PDF). The PDF has the older version, Figure 1.13 on [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=55|p.55]]
> **What it shows:** the general model of operations and process management, updated with **data**.
> - **Left:** transformed resources (materials, information, customers) and transforming resources (people, facilities **and data**) enter the operation. **Suppliers** sit on the far left, **customers** on the far right, and outputs are products and services.
> - **Centre:** a cycle of the 4 Ds around the operations managers: **Direct** (steering operations processes) → **Design** (shaping operations and processes) → **Deliver** (planning and controlling ongoing activity) → **Develop** (improving capabilities) → back to Direct.
> - **The new element:** in the middle, operations managers use **analytics** for operations decision-making. Binary-code icons show **data flowing in from suppliers, customers, external sources and internal sources**. The arrow "data becomes a transforming resource" says that data is no longer just something processed (information as a transformed resource): it now *drives* decisions in all four Ds.
> **How to explain it:** "Compared with the classic model, data has joined people and facilities as a transforming resource. It feeds analytics that support every D, which is the 'smart operations' idea behind the course title."
