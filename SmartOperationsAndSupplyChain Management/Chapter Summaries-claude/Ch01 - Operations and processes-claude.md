# Chapter 1 – Operations and processes

> [!abstract] In one breath (for the oral)
> Every organisation has **operations**, because every organisation produces some mix of products and services. Operations are made of **processes**, and every process is an **input → transformation → output** system. Processes are connected in **networks** at three levels (supply network, operation, process). What makes processes different is not the sector they are in but their **4 Vs** (volume, variety, variation, visibility). Operations contribute to the enterprise in five ways (**cost, revenue, risk, investment, capabilities**) and are judged on five objectives (**quality, speed, dependability, flexibility, cost**), all framed by the **triple bottom line**. Operations managers' work falls into the **4 Ds**: direct, design, deliver, develop.

## Executive summary of the book (the 5 diagnostic questions)
[[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=25|p.25–26]]
1. **Does the organisation understand the potential of operations and process management?** The operations function produces products/services, and *every* organisation has one. Processes are its component parts, and every function has processes.
2. **Does the organisation take a process perspective?** Understand the business through its processes, at three levels (supply network, operation, process). Process boundaries are whatever we define them to be, e.g. **end-to-end processes**.
3. **Does operations and process management have a strategic impact?** Yes, through cost, revenue, risk, investment and capabilities. "All failures are ultimately process failures."
4. **Are processes managed to reflect their operating circumstances?** Not necessarily: processes differ in the **4 Vs**, yet managers use a common set of activities (**4 Ds**).
5. **Is operations decision-making appropriate?** Decisions fall into 4 areas with overlaps. Managers use **qualitative and quantitative models**, but models simplify reality, which is why there is interest in **behavioural operations management**.

## 1.1 Operations and process management

**Key definitions** (the same four that open the chapter and Vinelli's slides):

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Operations and process management** | Managing the resources and processes that produce products and services, for **internal and external** customers. |
| **Operations management** | How the *operations function* produces products and services for **external** customers. |
| **Process** | An arrangement of resources and activities that transforms inputs into outputs that satisfy (internal or external) customer needs. |
| **Operations** | Transform inputs into outputs. **All operations are composed of processes.** |

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=31|p.31]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: All operations are composed of processes.

An operations manager may have another title: *fleet manager* (logistics), *administrative manager* (hospital), *store manager* (supermarket). The book uses "operation", "operations function" and "operations" interchangeably ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=27|p.27]]).

### All enterprises have operations
- The operations function is the part of the organisation that produces products or services. Since **every** organisation produces something, every organisation has operations.
- *"If you think that you don't have an operations function, you are wrong. If you think that your operations function is not important, you are also wrong."* In most enterprises it represents **the bulk of its assets and the majority of its people**.
- An effective operations function can **survive a turbulent environment** and keep improving steadily. A poorly managed one *always* prevents the organisation from reaching its objectives, whether social or economic.
- **Image problem:** operations is sometimes seen as routine, technical and low-level. Wrong: *"'Operations' is how one makes things happen […] it is through one's operations that strategy is made into reality."*
- 🎓 Class example: **H&M produces 0% of the clothes it sells**, yet it is full of operations managers: it designs, sources, distributes and sells. The *production* step is outsourced, the *operations* are not.

### Not all operations are the same

![[file-20260930184220550.png]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 1.2 – Different operations inside the same sector · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=28|p.28]]
> - **Rows = sectors** (financial services, furniture, hotels). **Columns = market position.**
> - Left column: value-for-money and high volume, competing on **cost** (bank call centre, mass kitchen units, budget hotel). Right column: upmarket and customised, competing on **specification/customisation** (investment advisor, craft furniture, luxury hotel).
> - **Takeaway:** two operations in the *same sector* can differ more than two operations in *different sectors*. Market position (volume, variety, how you compete) drives how operations must be managed, **not** the sector label.

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=27|p.27]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: The economic sector of an operation is less important in determining how it should be managed than its intrinsic characteristics.

### History: from production management to operations and supply chain management

![[file-20260930184700503.png]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 1.3 – How the scope of the subject grew · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=30|p.30]]
> Read the cube as **three expansions** starting from the small pink cube at the bottom-left, which stands for "production management of physical goods":
> 1. **Type of operation** (horizontal axis): from manufactured goods only → **all goods and services**.
> 2. **Type of process** (depth axis): from core production processes → **all processes in all functions** (marketing, finance, HR also run processes).
> 3. **Interaction between operations** (vertical axis): from one operation's internal processes → **networks and chains of operations**.
> The big pink cube at the top-right is today's **operations and supply chain management**.

The four "names" of the subject, in order:
1. **Production / manufacturing management.** Only factories.
2. **Operations management.** The ideas also apply to services, and the scope widens to related processes (purchasing, distribution, after-sales, product development).
3. **Operations and process management.** *Every* function is made of processes.
4. **Operations and supply chain management.** What happens outside the company's boundary (suppliers, customers) matters as much as what happens inside. The boundary itself is a decision: make or buy (→ Chapter 4). *"No operation does everything itself; some degree of outsourcing is inevitable."* ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=31|p.31]])

- The term "operations management" spread in the **1970s–80s** for the two reasons in point 2 ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=28|p.28]]).
- **Operations vs processes:** the difference is one of **scale, and therefore complexity**. Both transform inputs into outputs, but processes are the smaller version, the **building blocks** of an operation. Examples: marketing "produces" plans and forecasts, accounting "produces" budgets, HR "produces" recruitment plans ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=30|p.30]]).

## 1.2 The process perspective

**Other ways to describe an organisation** ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=32|p.32]]): its organisational structure (reporting lines), how it makes decisions, its culture (values, rituals), or its power relationships. **None gives the total picture**, and they are not mutually exclusive. The book uses the process perspective because it **directly links the way resources are managed with strategic impact**: *"Without effective process management, the best strategic plan can never become reality."*

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=32|p.32]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: There are many valid approaches to describing organisations. The process perspective is a particularly valuable one.

> [!tip] Core idea
> An organisation is a **collection of interconnected processes**. Each function (marketing, finance, HR…) has its own technical knowledge, but also an **operations role**: it uses processes to produce *plans, policies, reports and services* for internal customers. So **every manager is, to some extent, an operations manager**.

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=33|p.33]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: All processes have inputs of transforming and transformed resources that they use to create products and services.

### The input–transformation–output model

![[file-20260930194217059.png]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 1.4 – Input → transformation → output · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=33|p.33]]
> - **Transformed resources** (top-left) are what gets *changed*: **materials, information, customers**.
> - **Transforming resources** (bottom-left) are what *does the changing*: **people and facilities** (buildings, equipment, technology). They are the "building blocks" of every process.
> - The output is **products and services** for customers.
> - Bank example: printing statements processes **materials**, account management processes **information**, branch advice processes **customers**.

- For a bank, the **information-transforming** processes are probably the most important: a clunky website annoys us, but an error in our transactions hurts us far more fundamentally ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=34|p.34]]).
- **Facilities and people differ between processes:** a five-star hotel's facilities are mainly buildings and furniture; a nuclear aircraft carrier's are a reactor, turbines and electronics. Food-plant staff may need low technical skill, auditors very high skill. **Everyone contributes**: a food worker ignoring hygiene damages the business as surely as an accountant who can't add up.

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=34|p.34]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: Transformed resource inputs to a process are materials, information or customers. · All processes have transforming resources of facilities (equipment, technology, etc.) and people.

### Front office and back office

![[Pasted image 20260930195328.png]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 1.5 – Front office vs back office · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=34|p.34]]
> When the transformed resource is the **customer**, split the operation in two:
> - **Front office** (pink, bottom): processes that act *on customers*, so **high customer visibility**.
> - **Back office** (blue, top): processes that support the front office (materials, information), so **low customer visibility**.
> People and facilities feed both. Why it matters: front-office processes must manage customer perception and waiting; back-office processes can be optimised for efficiency. The boundary between them **is not clean**: processes have different degrees of exposure (visibility).

### The output: serving customers
Outputs must satisfy **customers**:
- **External customers** pay, so they are the organisation's reason for being. They have **freedom of choice**: fail them repeatedly and they leave.
- **Internal customers** (other processes, individuals or groups) usually **can't go elsewhere** in the short term (you can't choose another payroll process). In the long run they matter just as much: poor internal service eventually becomes poor external service, higher costs, or both ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=35|p.35–36]]).

### SIPOC analysis
[[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=36|p.36]] · Figure 1.6
**S**uppliers, **I**nputs, **P**rocess, **O**utputs, **C**ustomers: a first, general formalisation of a process built on the input–transformation–output model. The book's example is an HR **recruitment process**:
- *Suppliers:* functional head, project manager, recruitment agency.
- *Inputs:* recruitment request, long-term resourcing plan, labour-market reports.
- *Process:* agree the essentials → authorise the budget → contract the agency → agree a shortlist → make an offer and negotiate conditions → confirm the start date.
- *Outputs:* a qualified new staff member, completed staff records, payment authorisation.
- *Customers:* functional head, project manager, the new recruit.
**Why it's useful:** everyone understands *and agrees* what the process involves and where it fits. It also prompts the questions that are often overlooked: what information should suppliers give, with what notice and in what form? Who is responsible for each step? Who must be informed when it's done? How is success judged?

### Three levels of analysis

![[Pasted image 20261001133737.png]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 1.7 – Three levels of analysis · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=37|p.37]]
> Think of it as zooming in with a camera:
> 1. **Supply network** (top): flow *between operations*. You need to know each operation's role and how they relate. This is **strategic** analysis.
> 2. **Operation** (middle): flow *between processes* inside one operation.
> 3. **Process** (bottom): flow *between resources* (people and facilities) inside one process. This is **operational** analysis.
> The same input–transformation–output logic applies at every zoom level.

So operations and process management rests on two ideas: **(1)** the input–transformation–output model, and **(2)** processes connected in **networks**.

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=37|p.37]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: A process perspective can be used at three levels: the level of the operation itself, the level of the supply network and the level of individual processes.

### Level 1: the operation
The operation as a whole is an input–transformation–output system. Note that in some operations the **customers themselves are inputs** (airline, department store, police), which gives them visibility of the processes. **Table 1.1** ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=38|p.38]]; transformed inputs are in italics in the book):

| Operation | Inputs | Some processes | Outputs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Airline | Aircraft, pilots/crew, ground crew, *passengers, cargo* | Check-in, baggage drop, security, boarding, flying, scheduling, in-flight care, transfer, reclaim | Transported passengers and freight |
| Department store | *Goods for sale*, staff, registers, *customers* | Source merchandise, manage inventory, display, sales advice, aftercare, complaints, delivery | Customers and goods "assembled" together |
| Police department | Officers, computer systems, *information*, *public* | Crime prevention and detection, information gathering, victim support, charging/detaining, custody, liaising with courts | Lawful society; public with a feeling of security |
| Frozen-food manufacturer | *Fresh food*, operators, processing equipment, freezers | Source raw materials, input checks, prepare, assemble, pack, fast-freeze, quality checks, finished-goods inventory | Frozen food |

### Products or services? (IHIP)
[[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=38|p.38–40]]
A **product** is physical and tangible. A **service** is an activity that usually involves interaction with a customer (or something representing them). The classic distinction is **IHIP**:

| Characteristic | Meaning | Operational consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **I**ntangibility | Not a physical item | Hard to define the "boundary" of the service, so **manage customer expectations** |
| **H**eterogeneity | Hard to standardise; each delivery differs because customers differ | Unpredictable requests, cost efficiency is hard, staff must be trained for variety |
| **I**nseparability | Production and consumption are simultaneous | Capacity must be in place **when demand occurs**; customer guidance (e.g. FAQs) can reduce contact |
| **P**erishability | Can't be stored (a theatre performance perishes as it is created) | **Match capacity and demand**, or you get idle resources or lost revenue; forecasting helps |

There are problems with IHIP: many services don't fit it, and **ICT** has changed how far it applies. But each characteristic has *some* validity.

**Figure 1.8** ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=39|p.39]]) is a spectrum from "pure" product to "pure" service: **mineral extraction** (tangible, standardised, storable, produced away from consumption) → **canned food** (similar, but supermarkets also buy *information* about demand and delivery) → **cloud-based IT service** (few tangible elements, some customisation, can store preferences) → **psychotherapy clinic** (meets all IHIP characteristics).

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=40|p.40]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: Most operations produce a blend of tangible products and intangible services.

**Services and products are merging** ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=40|p.40–41]]):
- **Service-dominant logic** (Vargo & Lusch): every business exists to *serve customers*. Service is the fundamental basis of exchange, physical goods are only *distribution mechanisms* for service, and **the customer is always a co-creator of value**.
- **Co-creation / co-production:** customers take part in creating value (in the supermarket they pick, scan, pack and transport; patients describe symptoms). The line between producer and consumer blurs, which requires two-way, transparent interaction.
- **Servitisation:** shift the focus from the product to the **services the product enables**. Classic example: **Rolls-Royce "power by the hour"**, where airlines pay per engine flying hour, priced on the support needed and the performance guaranteed (availability, repair turnaround, reliability). Why firms do it: **extra revenue** from the installed base, **more stable revenue** (resistant to economic cycles), **closer customer relationships** and a better understanding of needs that feeds back into product and service design.

### Level 2: the supply network
[[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=42|p.42–43]]
Every operation has suppliers and customers and competes with others that share some of them. This collection is the **supply network**. Three issues:
1. **It can be complex:** many customers and suppliers, and subtle relationships (a competitor in one market can be a collaborator or supplier in another).
2. **Its boundaries can be very wide:** from the mine to reuse/disposal (needed for sustainability studies), but usually a boundary is set to focus on the closer operations.
3. **It is always changing:** customers and suppliers are won and lost, acquired and sold.
**The key question:** *"Does my operation make a contribution to the supply network as a whole?"* Are we a good **customer** (easy to do business with, lowering the long-term cost of supply)? Are we a good **supplier** (we understand customers' needs and have the capability to satisfy them)? → covered strategically in Ch 4 and operationally in Ch 7.

### Level 3: the individual process
[[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=43|p.43]]
Every operation is a network of processes, each one an **internal supplier and internal customer**. If the operation isn't working, trace the problem back along this internal chain. **Table 1.2** gives processes in non-operations functions:

| Function | Processes | Outputs | Customers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Marketing and sales | Planning, forecasting, order-taking | Marketing plans, forecasts, confirmed orders | Senior mgmt, sales staff, planners, operations, finance |
| Finance and accounting | Budgeting, capital approval, invoicing | Budgets, capital evaluations, invoices | Everyone, senior mgmt, external customers |
| HRM | Payroll, recruitment, training | Salary statements, new hires, trained employees | Employees, all other processes |
| IT | Systems review, help desk, implementation projects | Evaluations, advice, working systems | All other processes |

Implication: organisational **boundaries between functions are secondary**. Firms constantly move tasks between departments, while the tasks and processes themselves change less often. Processes can also move between businesses: that is outsourcing ("do or buy").

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=43|p.43]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: Whole businesses, and even whole supply networks, can be viewed as networks of processes.

### The 'line of sight'
[[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=44|p.44]]
People in each process should have a clear **line of sight forward** to the external customers their performance affects (so they see how they add value and can help the processes between them and the customer), and **backwards** to understand what they need from internal and external suppliers.

### Process boundaries are a choice: end-to-end processes
How processes are defined is **not predetermined**. Commonly they are grouped by type of activity (invoicing, design, sales, assembly), which is convenient because similar resources sit together. That is only one option. **End-to-end** processes group all the activities and resources that satisfy *one defined customer need*. This is the idea behind **business process re-engineering** (Ch 12): a radical rethink that moves resources out of functions.

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=44|p.44]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: Processes are defined by how the organisation chooses to draw process boundaries.

### End-to-end business processes

![[Pasted image 20261001134014.png]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 1.10 – Reorganising around end-to-end processes (Studio Division example) · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=46|p.46]]
> - **Top row:** the traditional, *functional* organisation (client relationship → set & props → engineering → production → planning → finance).
> - **Red arrows:** *end-to-end* processes that start from a customer need and end when it is fulfilled (pitch preparation, programme production, advertising contracts, technical support).
> - **Circles:** how much each function contributes to each end-to-end process (bigger circle = bigger contribution).

## 1.3 Does operations and process management have a strategic impact?

- **Don't confuse "operations" with "operational".** *Operational* means detailed, local, short-term, day-to-day (the opposite of strategic). *Operations* is the set of resources that produce products and services, and it can be treated at both the operational and the strategic level ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=46|p.46]]).
- Operations can **"make" the business** (short-term responsiveness and long-term capabilities) or **"break" it** (if it can't produce effectively, no positioning or selling will save it).

**Three levels of operations performance** ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=47|p.47]]):
1. **Societal** (long-term issues, stakeholders, the TBL);
2. **Enterprise** (support for strategic aims: the 5 contributions);
3. **Process** (how well processes serve internal and external customers: the 5 objectives).

**Stakeholders** (employees, customers, shareholders, community, society) all care about operations performance, but each values different aspects.

### Triple bottom line (people, planet, profit)

> [!PDF|red] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=47|p.47]]
> One idea that tries to capture the idea of a broader approach to assessing an organisation's performance is the 'triple bottom line' (TBL, or 3BL), also known as 'people, planet and profit'. […] A sustainable business is one that creates an acceptable profit for its owners, but minimises the damage to the environment and enhances the existence of the people with whom it has contact. In other words, it balances economic, environmental and societal interests. This gives the organisation its 'license to operate' in society.

> [!important] OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE
> Operations should judge themselves on the triple bottom line principle of 'people, planet and profit'. ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=47|p.47]])

- The phrase was coined by **John Elkington** (1994, SustainAbility). The underlying assumption, *not universally accepted*, is that a sustainable business is more likely to succeed in the long term.
- **Critics** ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=47|p.47]]): it is unreasonable to ask managers to maximise profit *and* serve society at the same time. **Michael Jensen** (Harvard) asks: *"How do we want the firms in our economy to measure their own performance?"*. He argues the stakeholder view gives undue weight to narrow special interests and a spurious legitimacy that *"undermines the foundations of value-seeking behaviour"*.

### Operations performance at the level of the enterprise
It now attracts far more attention, and reportedly **the largest share of consultancy spending**, partly because it was neglected in the past. It has both short- and long-term impact ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=48|p.48]]).

### The five ways operations contribute to the enterprise

| Contribution | How operations deliver it |
|---|---|
| **Cost** | Efficiency: the more productive the transformation, the lower the cost per unit. The higher the cost relative to the market price, the more important cost reduction becomes. |
| **Revenue** | Quality, service and innovation keep customers and attract new ones. Operations directly influence quality, speed, dependability and flexibility. |
| **Risk** | Well-run operations fail less: *all failures can eventually be traced back to some kind of failure within a process*. When they fail they recover faster (**resilience**). |
| **Investment** | Lower capital needed *per unit of output*, by raising effective capacity and using resources innovatively. |
| **Capabilities** | Each unit produced is a chance to learn. The knowledge accumulated is the basis of future innovation. |

> [!PDF|note] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=48|p.48]]
> It can build capabilities that will form the basis for future innovation by building a solid base of operations skills and knowledge within the business. Every time an operation produces a product or a service it has the opportunity to accumulate knowledge about how that product or service is best produced.

🎓 Class example on **capabilities**: why do luxury brands like **Bottega Veneta** keep production in Italy? Because that is where the craft capabilities are concentrated: the people who know how to make the product.

> [!important] OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE (to mention at the exam)
> All operations should be expected to contribute to their enterprise by controlling costs, increasing revenue, reducing risk, making investment more effective and growing long-term capabilities. ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=48|p.48]])

## 1.5 Diagnostic question: are processes managed to reflect their operating circumstances? The 4 Vs

Processes differ in **technical** content, and also in the **nature of their demand**, which the 4 Vs describe:
- **Volume:** how many.
- **Variety:** how many different products or services.
- **Variation in demand:** how much demand fluctuates and how predictable it is.
- **Visibility:** how much of the process the customer experiences.

Details and book examples ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=50|p.50–52]]):
- **Volume:** high repeatability → staff **specialise** → **systemisation** (standard procedures in a manual) → specialised, efficient technology → **low unit cost**. Book example: **McDonald's/KFC** vs a small local diner.
- **Variety:** many activities and frequent changes → general-purpose skills and technology, wide inputs, complexity of matching customer needs → **higher cost**. Book example: a **taxi** (infinite possible routes) costs more per km than a **bus**.
- **Variation in demand:** constant demand can be planned with just enough resources. Variable or unpredictable demand needs resources adjusted over time and a **capacity cushion**. Book example: **high-fashion garments** (seasonality plus uncertainty about popular styles) vs **business suits**.
- **Visibility:** processes that act on customers (retail, healthcare) are more visible. Even material and information processes can add some visibility (**parcel track-and-trace**). Low visibility allows a **time lag** (days) and a "factory-like" process with high utilisation. High visibility requires **customer-contact skills** and costs more. Book example: an **airport** has high-visibility processes (check-in, information, restaurants, passport control) and low-visibility ones (baggage handling, freight, meal loading, cleaning).

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=50|p.50]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: The way in which processes need to be managed is influenced by volume, variety, variation in demand and visibility.

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791192524661.webp]]

> [!info] 📖 Figure 1.11 – Implications of the 4 Vs · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=52|p.52]]
> Each arrow is one V. **Right side = the low-cost end; left side = the high-cost end.**
> - **Volume high →** repeatability, specialisation, systemisation, capital-intensive technology, **low unit cost**. (Low volume: each person does more of the job, so high unit cost.)
> - **Variety low →** well defined, routine, standardised, **low unit cost**. (High variety: flexible but complex, high cost.)
> - **Variation low →** stable, predictable, high utilisation, **low unit cost**. (High variation: capacity must keep changing, which needs anticipation and spare capacity.)
> - **Visibility low →** time lag between production and consumption, standardised, centralised, **low unit cost**. (High visibility: short waiting tolerance, customer perception matters, contact skills needed.)
> **One-line rule:** high volume and low variety, variation and visibility mean low cost.
> Note: volume is drawn with "low" on the **left**, unlike the others, so that all the low-cost ends sit on the right.

**Where each V matters later in the book** ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=53|p.53]]): volume and variety → resourcing and design (Ch 4–5); variation → capacity management (Ch 8); visibility → how quality is judged (Ch 13).

🎓 Class examples: **Fincantieri** has very low volume (one ship at a time, highly customised), while **McDonald's** has high volume because it has low variety, which allows a standardised chain of work.

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=53|p.53]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: Operations and processes can (other things being equal) reduce their costs by increasing volume, reducing variety, reducing variation and reducing visibility.

### Charting processes with the 4 Vs: retail banking

> [!info] 📖 Figure 1.12 – 4 V profiles of retail banking processes · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=53|p.53]]
> Each coloured line is one process, crossing the four arrows:
> - **Personal banker/advisor** (dark blue, left): low volume, high variety, high variation, high visibility. This is the **most expensive** profile, so it is offered only to wealthy, high-profit clients.

## 1.6 The operations manager's activities: the 4 Ds

| D | Activity |
|---|---|
| **Direct** | Set the overall operations strategy: strategic purpose, innovation, what to keep in-house vs outsource. |
| **Design** | Determine the physical form, shape and composition of operations and processes and their resources. |
| **Deliver** | Plan and control the flow from suppliers, through the operation, to customers. |
| **Develop** | Improve process capabilities and performance over time. |

> [!PDF|yellow] [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=54|p.54]]
> OPERATIONS PRINCIPLE: Operations management activities can be grouped into four broad categories: directing the overall strategy of the operation; designing the operation's products, services and processes; planning and controlling delivery; and developing process performance.

The 4 Ds roughly follow the **life cycle** of operations and processes. The boundaries between them are **not clean**: directing affects everything, design limits delivery and development, and delivery choices (e.g. lean) shape improvement. *All operations decisions are interrelated.*

**Figure 1.13** ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=55|p.55]]) combines the two ideas (transformation model + 4 Ds) and maps them onto the book's chapters: **Direct** = Ch 1–4 · **Design** = Ch 5–6 · **Deliver** = Ch 7–11 (supply chain, capacity, inventory, planning & control, lean) · **Develop** = Ch 12–15 (improvement, quality, risk & resilience, project management).

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791193380459.webp|700]]

> [!example] 🎓 Lecture photo – Figure 1.14 of the newer edition (not in the PDF). The PDF has the older version, Figure 1.13 on [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=55|p.55]]
> **What it shows:** the general model of operations and process management, updated with **data**.
> - **Left:** transformed resources (materials, information, customers) and transforming resources (people, facilities **and data**) enter the operation. **Suppliers** sit on the far left, **customers** on the far right, and outputs are products and services.
> - **Centre:** a cycle of the 4 Ds around the operations managers: **Direct** (steering operations processes) → **Design** (shaping operations and processes) → **Deliver** (planning and controlling ongoing activity) → **Develop** (improving capabilities) → back to Direct.
> - **The new element:** in the middle, operations managers use **analytics** for operations decision-making. Binary-code icons show **data flowing in from suppliers, customers, external sources and internal sources**. The arrow "data becomes a transforming resource" says that data is no longer just something processed (information as a transformed resource): it now *drives* decisions in all four Ds.
> **How to explain it:** "Compared with the classic model, data has joined people and facilities as a transforming resource. It feeds analytics that support every D, which is the 'smart operations' idea behind the course title."

### Models in operations decision-making
[[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=56|p.56–57]]
- A **model** is *"an explicit statement of our image of reality"*: a simplified, organised abstraction that may leave out or aggregate factors.
- **Qualitative models** describe relationships without precise values (e.g. the Studio Division figure). They don't give an "answer" but stimulate understanding.
- **Quantitative models** use mathematics or statistics (e.g. **EOQ**, Ch 9). Their risk is that reality has to be simplified so much that usefulness is limited. In practice, **combine both**.
- **Behavioural operations management (BOM)** studies how human behaviour interacts with operational systems, and challenges the assumption that managers are rational. Unrealistic assumptions hidden in many models: (1) people are not a major factor; (2) they decide independently of each other; (3) they don't learn from experience; (4) they are not part of the product/service; (5) work is perfectly observable. The book's models are therefore meant to **structure and clarify** decisions, not prescribe one optimum.

### Critical commentary
[[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=57|p.57]]
- Some, especially **professionals**, say calling everything a "process" dehumanises the organisation (a European doctors' union complained of a *"sausage factory service based on productivity targets"*; "a hospital can never be viewed as a factory").
- The book's reply: this rests on the false idea that **people and process are different**. A process is simply "how you do things", and people are its most important resource. A purely mechanistic view is dangerous, but the risk is worth taking.

### Summary checklist (book)
[[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=58|p.58]]: Is the operations function clearly defined? Do managers know they are operations managers? Do non-operations functions realise they manage processes? Are the inputs, activities and outputs understood? Is the product/service balance understood? Is the impact on environment and society assessed? What does operations contribute to cost, revenue, risk, capital and capability? Is the position in the supply network understood? Are internal customer and supplier concepts used? Have end-to-end processes been considered? Are the 4 V differences understood and reflected in how processes are managed? Are managers aware of all decisions and of the qualitative and quantitative models?

## 📚 Case examples and worked examples (Chapter 1)

> [!example] Case – **Torchbox** (web design) · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=31|p.31–32]]
> **Facts:** independent UK web design and development company founded in 2000 by Tom Dyson and Olly Willans; on Employee Ownership Day 2019 ownership passed to the employees. Its clients come mainly from the charity, NGO and public sectors and share its commitment to sustainability and ethics. Being small makes it flexible and agile, and senior managers can't be too specialised. Clients want accessibility, usability, performance and security, **on time and on budget**. It uses a *feature-driven development* process with **kanban** project management: *"every hour we work produces an hour's worth of value"*.
> **Why it's in the chapter:** even a creative, fast-moving industry needs **operations management**. Operational effectiveness is essential to success, so "operations" is not just factories.
> **Oral use:** an example of "all organisations have operations" and of a professional or knowledge service run with OM techniques.

> [!example] Case – **Marina Bay Sands** hotel, Singapore · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=35|p.35]]
> **Facts:** a luxury resort with 2,500+ rooms, a convention centre, shopping mall, museum, theatres and casino, topped by the SkyPark with its infinity pool. Behind the front office: chefs hand-make **5,000 dim sum a day**; the laundry handles tens of thousands of items daily (4,000 pool towels a day), and **over £10 million** was invested in water-saving technology (**−70% water**). The high-tech wardrobe has **18 automated conveyors** with 620 slots each and chip-tracked uniforms for 9,000+ staff. Processes that straddle the divide: **valet parking** (up to 200 cars an hour, 2,500 spaces, **7-minute** retrieval target), housekeeping and **50 butlers** (one arranged a wedding banquet at four hours' notice).
> **Why:** the best illustration of **front office vs back office**. Customers judge the front office, but it depends on invisible back-office processes, which are mass operations in their own right.
> **Oral use:** Fig 1.5, visibility (4 Vs), and back-office sustainability (TBL).

> [!example] Case – **Servitisation and circular design at Philips Lighting** · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=41|p.41–42]]
> **Facts:** **Lighting-as-a-Service (LaaS)**: Philips designs, installs, operates and maintains the lighting, and the customer **pays only for the light used** ("pay-per-lux"). It started with architect **Thomas Rau** (RAU Architects, Amsterdam), who didn't want to buy an over-engineered system and later dispose of it. The solution was a minimalist light plan using daylight, with sensors that dim or brighten. At **Schiphol Airport**, Philips **keeps ownership** of fixtures and is responsible for performance, durability, reuse and recycling: **LEDs −50% electricity**, fixtures last **75% longer**, and components are replaced individually (less material, lower maintenance). (Philips Lighting is now called *Signify*.)
> **Why:** it combines **servitisation** with the **circular economy**. The supplier keeps control over the whole life cycle of the product.
> **Oral use:** products and services merging, service-dominant logic, and the TBL.

> [!example] Worked example – **Studio Division (SD)** of a broadcaster, three levels of analysis · Figure 1.9, [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=44|p.44–45]]
> - **Supply network:** SD specialises in a few high-growth types of work (less dependence on its own channels). It **outsources CGI and post-production**; its design workshop was so successful it was **spun out** as a division serving other companies too. In the network: broadcasters A/B, channel X, competitors.
> - **Operation:** it produces media files, but the real output is creativity and "artistry", interpreting the client's needs thanks to skilled staff and state-of-the-art technology.
> - **Processes:** planning and traffic (the internal "operations managers"), set and props workshops, client liaison, engineering, production units, finance and costing.
> - **End-to-end redesign** (Fig 1.10): regroup resources into **business processes that fulfil defined customer needs** (pitch preparation, programme production, promotional/advertising contracts, technical support), shown by the dotted lines.
> **Second part** ([[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=49|p.49]]), strategic impact: budget reviews and yearly efficiency gains (**cost**); "we are not the cheapest, but we are the best", on time and creative (**revenue**); robust processes (**risk**); justifying equipment spend to the board (**investment**); integrating new tech into creative processes (**capabilities**).
> **Oral use:** apply all the chapter's frameworks to one operation.

> [!example] Case – **Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF)** · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=50|p.50]]
> **Facts:** humanitarian medical aid in conflicts, epidemics, famines and disasters. It needs *"fast response, efficient logistics systems, and efficient project management"*. Its process has **5 phases: proposal → assessment → initiation → running → closing**. Experts evaluate the crisis; thanks to pre-planned processes, specialised kits and emergency stores it can **distribute material within 48 hours**. Projects last **18 months to 3.5 years**, and closure is a gradual withdrawal or a hand-over (or an early exit if staff safety is at risk).
> **Why:** OM also applies to **not-for-profit** organisations; good intentions need superior operations.

> [!example] Case – **Adidas shuts its robotic 'Speedfactories'** · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=55|p.55–56]]
> **Facts:** Adidas outsourced manufacturing to **1,000+ facilities in 63 countries**, mostly in Asia. Problems: rising costs, a complex network that was hard to control, **~18 months** from concept to shop, and 2–3 months for replenishment, while fashion cycles were shrinking. The **Speedfactories** (Germany and the US) were fully automated and close to the market, meant to make shoes in days and replenish best-sellers within the same season. Within **4 years** they closed: production moved back to Asia, where the know-how and most suppliers are (Adidas already makes >90% of products there), and the technology went to two Asian suppliers. The main cause was the **restricted range**: only knit uppers with the "Boost" sole, not leather with rubber soles (a different joining process).
> **Why:** the objective was right, but **insufficient attention to the manufacturing process** made it fail. Innovative strategy still needs sound process decisions, and the learning was reused.
> **Oral use:** links Direct and Design decisions, flexibility vs automation (Ch 5), location and outsourcing (Ch 4).

> [!example] End-of-chapter case study – **KTS services** (Kaston-Trenton Service) · [[Operations-and-Process-Management-–-Gestión-por-Procesos.pdf#page=59|p.59–61]]
> **Facts:** a boiler maintenance firm in the East of England run by siblings Ros (marketing, sales, finance) and Mark (operations, supply), with 42 technicians (26 domestic, 16 business) and a 4-person office. Three businesses:
> - **Domestic servicing:** a "cash cow", price-sensitive, **95% renewal**. **20% of visits are call-outs**, unpredictable in timing and duration. Customers need flexible appointments, absolute reliability and a robust repair.
> - **Business servicing:** customised systems, so higher variety; **25–30% call-outs**. Customers want advice, information, reports (efficiency, downtime) and fast response. It is more profitable.
> - **Design and installation:** new, customised systems, satisfied customers, but **loses money** (workshop, expensive under-used designer).
> Volumes (Table 1.3): domestic 7,331 → 9,700 contracts; business 972 → 1,354; installation 3 → 6.
> **Mark's efficiency plan:** technicians waste **up to 30%** of their time on non-value-adding activities. Remedies: KPIs, better forecasting (seasonality, weather), slicker processes, better dispatching (not first-come-first-served), better training. **Future:** a "total" heating service (the customer outsources heating; pay per energy unit or for a guaranteed temperature?), and decarbonisation (gas boilers possibly banned in new homes, hydrogen, heat pumps, insulation).
> **Book questions and answer hints:**
> 1. *4 Vs:* domestic = high volume, low variety, medium variation (call-outs), high visibility at home; business = lower volume, higher variety and variation, higher visibility (customers want involvement); installation = very low volume, very high variety.
> 2. *Objectives to win servicing:* price (domestic), dependability (appointments), speed (call-outs), quality (robustness of the repair), information and contact (business).
> 3. *Total service:* servitisation, like Philips LaaS: stable revenue and a closer relationship, but energy and performance risk.
> 4. *Long term:* build capabilities in heat pumps and low-carbon systems, train staff (inside-out, Ch 2).

> [!note] Book exercises ("Applying the principles", p.62–63), with the logic of each answer
> - **Quentin Cakes:** *Custom Cake* (1,000/yr, high variety, high variation, peaks at celebrations, high visibility of the message) vs *Individual Cake* (4,000/yr, 10–15 types, demand more even). Use the 4 Vs: Individual is closer to the current profile (higher volume, lower variation). Custom adds variety, variation and peaks coinciding with Christmas, when capacity is already tight.
> - **Pret A Manger:** a fresh kitchen in every shop, unsold food given to charity, owned shops, people as "heart and soul". They do understand OM (quality focus in all activities, they rejected a central factory to protect freshness). Map their activities onto the 4 Ds.
> - **Focus Plastics:** big multi-cavity moulds (18 items/min vs 3) are efficient but take **3 hours to change**. With high variety and small batches, it ends up **making the wrong products in big batches**, so finished stock grows *and* stockouts happen. A mismatch between rigid technology and high variety and variation.
> - **Focus design-house partnerships:** lower volume, high variety, and **low visibility** for Focus (customers see the design-house brand, not Focus), with distribution provided by Focus's own resources.


> [!question] 🎤 Likely oral questions, with a one-line answer each
> - *Why does every organisation have operations?* Because every organisation produces a mix of products and services (e.g. H&M without factories).
> - *Is sector or market position more important?* Market position (Fig 1.2).
> - *Transformed vs transforming resources?* Materials/information/customers vs people/facilities (Fig 1.4).
> - *What are the 4 Vs and their cost implications?* Fig 1.11: high volume and low variety, variation and visibility mean low cost. Show the banking example (Fig 1.12).
> - *How do operations contribute to the enterprise?* Cost, revenue, risk, investment, capabilities, plus the TBL.
> - *What is the 4D model and what is new about it?* Direct, design, deliver, develop, with data as a transforming resource (lecture figure).
