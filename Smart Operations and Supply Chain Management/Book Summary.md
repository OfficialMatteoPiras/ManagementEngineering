# Chapter 1 — Operations and Process Management

## 1.1 The foundational definitions

The chapter opens with the most important idea of the course: **every organization produces something**. Every product you use and every service you experience comes to you courtesy of the operations managers who organized its production — even if they are not called that. They are the people who *design, run and improve the processes* that produce services and products for their customers.

The four definitions to know by heart:

| Term                                        | Definition                                                                                                                       |
| ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Operations and process management (OPM)** | the activity of managing the resources and processes that produce products and services, for **internal and external** customers |
| **Operations management (OM)**              | examines how the operations *function* produces products and services for **external** customers                                 |
| **Process**                                 | an arrangement of resources and activities that transforms inputs into outputs that satisfy internal or external customer needs  |
| **Operations**                              | transform inputs into outputs. All operations are composed of processes                                                          |

🎯 **Note the OPM vs OM difference**: it is the same shift that runs through the whole chapter — OPM widens the lens from the operations *function* alone (external customers) to **all the processes of all the functions** (internal customers too). It is the historical move that Figure 1.3 tells with the pink cube.

## 1.2 All enterprises have operations

![[file-20260930184220550.png]]

*🖼️ **Figure 1.2** — Three sectors (financial services, furniture manufacturing, hotels), two photos each: on the left "value-for-money" operators competing largely on cost (an account management centre at a large retail bank, mass production of kitchen units, value-for-money hotel), on the right "upmarket" operators competing on high specification and customisation (investment bank advisor, craft 'antique' furniture, luxury hotel lobby).*

The figure is there to kill the intuition "sector = way of operating". All six operations have an operations function that produces the things their customers are willing to pay for — but **it is not the economic sector that makes them different from each other**:

- the three left-hand columns compete (largely) on **cost**;

- the three right-hand ones compete on some combination of high specification and **customisation**, with higher unit costs.

What really determines *how* an operation should be managed is not the sector but the **market position** it occupies and its **intrinsic characteristics**: the volume of its output, the variety of products and services it must produce and — above all — *how it is trying to compete* in its market.

> [!quote] 📖 Chapter 1

> "There are often bigger differences **within** economic sectors than **between** them. (…) the surface appearance of a business and its economic sector are less important to the way its operations should be managed than the intrinsic characteristics of what it is trying to achieve."

💡 This principle has a practical consequence that pays off in every chapter: when the exam asks "how would you manage operation X?", the answer **never starts from the sector** ("it's a bank, so…") but always from the operation's *profile* (volume, variety, variation, visibility → the 4Vs of section 1.7).

## 1.3 The history of the discipline: from production management to OSCM

![[file-20260930184700503.png]]

*🖼️ **Figure 1.3** — The pink cube of OM scope. Three expansion axes: (1) from physical goods to goods **and** services; (2) from core processes to **all processes** in all functions; (3) from the individual operation to **networks** of operations (supply networks and chains).*

The book tells the evolution in four stages (the name of the discipline changes with its scope):

1. **Production management** — originally the subject was almost exclusively associated with the manufacturing sector, and it would have been called 'production' or 'manufacturing' management.

2. **Operations management** — two trends: (a) ideas, approaches and techniques traditionally used in manufacturing proved equally applicable to the much larger service sector; (b) the scope of 'production' in manufacturing companies expanded to include 'non-core' but important production-related processes: purchasing, physical distribution, after-sales service, product development and so on.

3. **Operations and process management** — the conceptual jump: the production of products and services **is not confined to the operations function**. Every part of any business achieves its objectives by organizing its resources (people, information systems, buildings, equipment) into individual processes → the whole organization is made of processes.

4. **Operations and supply chain management** — the acceptance that operations *outside* the conventional boundary of the enterprise (the suppliers of goods and services) could have as much impact on performance as internal resources and processes. And the boundary itself is not fixed: it is determined by decisions made by the operation itself (make-or-buy, outsourcing → Chapter 4).

💡 **The H&M example** (from your notes): H&M produces nothing — no manufacturing — yet it is full of operations managers: design, procurement, retailing are all operations activities. Message: *"every company makes operations in a way or another"*. No company is "too much of a service" to have operations.

## 1.4 The process perspective

**Process perspective** = understanding that *every part of the business can be seen as a collection of processes*, and that *all processes can be managed using operations management principles*. The organization is a **collection of interrelated processes** that (hopefully) all contribute to fulfilling its strategic aims.

Powerful consequence: every function has its technical knowledge (marketing, finance…) but it also has an **operations role** — it uses its processes to *produce* plans, policies, reports and services. So **every manager, in every part of the organization, is to some extent an operations manager**: they should want to give good service to their internal customers, and do this efficiently.

🎯 The double track to remember: processes of the operations function (contributing *directly* to products/services) + processes of the other functions (managed with the *same principles*). It is the reason for the course name: *Operations **and Process** Management*.

### The input–transformation–output model

![[file-20260930194217059.png]]

*🖼️ **Figure 1.4** — All processes and operations are input–transformation–output systems: they use 'transforming' resources to work on 'transformed' resources in order to produce products and services.*

The central distinction is between the two kinds of input:

- **Transformed resources** (the resources that get transformed): **materials**, **information**, **customers**. E.g. a bank: one process prints account statements (= materials); in its branches, customers are processed by giving them financial advice (= customers).

- **Transforming resources** (the resources that do the transforming — the 'building blocks' of every process): **facilities** (the buildings, equipment, plant and process technology of the operation) and **people** (those who operate, maintain, plan and manage the operation).

💡 Why the order of errors matters: with a clunky website the customer is unhappy, but **errors in financial transactions hurt you in a far more fundamental way** — the quality of what is *transformed* outweighs the experience. A good illustration of why "quality" is defined as *fitness for purpose* of the product/service, not politeness.

### Front office and back office

![[Pasted image 20260930195328.png]]

*🖼️ Input-transformation-output of a service operation: resources enter, transformation happens in two zones — **back-office** processes (low customer visibility) supporting **front-office** processes (high visibility) — and the output is served customers.*

- **Front office** = the processes that interact with transforming customers; customers have **high visibility** of the operation's processes.

- **Back office** = the processes with little or no direct contact with customers, that support the front office; **low visibility** because customers receive the outputs without seeing inside the operation.

💡 This is the first taste of the fourth V (visibility, section 1.7): high visibility = customer contact skills, low waiting tolerance, high costs. The back office exists precisely to *hide* from the customer the high-volume, low-visibility processes.

### The output: products and services

All processes produce products and/or services. The distinction can be subtle, but in either case the outputs from processes and operations are (or should be) intended to serve the requirements of **customers**:

- **External customers** — provide the organization directly with its reason for being: they bring the revenue.

- **Internal customers** — serving them is, in the longer term, **just as important**: poor service to internal customers will eventually result in poor service to external customers, or excessive costs, or both.

### The three levels of analysis

![[Pasted image 20261001133737.png]]

*🖼️ **Figure 1.7** — OPM requires analysis at three levels: flows **between operations** (supply network level — strategic analysis), flows **between processes** (operation level) and flows **between resources** (process level — operational analysis).*

The input-transformation-output concept is the **first idea** that defines OPM; the second is that **all operations processes are connected in networks**. The network concept applies at three levels:

1. **The supply network level** — an operation among other operations: you need to know the role of each operation in the network and the relationships between them.

2. **The operation level** — an operation is an arrangement of processes: you need to know the role of each process in the operation and the relationships between them.

3. **The process level** — a process is an arrangement of resources (people and facilities): you need to know the role of each unit of resource and the relationships between them.

🎯 This framework is the skeleton of the whole book: Chapter 4 (see `Summary SOSCM - Macchion.md`) works at the supply network level; the process design chapters at the process level. In the exam it is the answer to "at what level are you analysing?".

### End-to-end processes

![[Pasted image 20261001134014.png]]

*🖼️ **Figure 1.10** — Studio Division example: how existing functionally-based processes (client relationship, set and props mfg., engineering, production units, planning and traffic, finance and costing) can be reorganised around **end-to-end business processes** (pitch preparation, programme production…) going from "customer needs" to "customer needs fulfilled". The circles show each function's contribution to each end-to-end process.*

💡 The figure anticipates a hot theme of the book: **functional silos** organize competences, but the customer does not buy functions — they buy the flow that crosses all of them. Thinking in end-to-end processes means asking *which horizontal chain of activities fulfils a stated customer need*, and only then which function contributes to each link. It is the same logic that Chapter 4 will scale up as the supply network, seen *inside* the organization.

## 1.5 Operations performance at the level of the enterprise

### Triple bottom line

> [!quote] 📖 p. 47
> "One idea that tries to capture the idea of a broader approach to assessing an organisation's performance is the **'triple bottom line'** (TBL, or 3BL), also known as **'people, planet and profit'**. (…) organisations should measure themselves not just on the traditional economic profit that they generate for their owners, but also on the impact their operations have on society (…) and the ecological impact on the environment. (…) A sustainable business is one that creates an acceptable profit for its owners, but minimises the damage to the environment and enhances the existence of the people with whom it has contact. (…) This gives the organisation its **'license to operate'** in society."

🎯 The operations principle (flagged "need to mention for the exam" in your notes, slide p. 12):

> **Operations Principle** — Operations should judge themselves on the triple bottom line principle of 'people, planet and profit'.

💡 The TBL is not (only) ethics: the book ties it to survival — the assumption is that a sustainable business is *more likely to remain successful in the long term* than one that focuses on economic goals alone. Note also the forward link: the TBL is the antecedent of the **stakeholders/CSR** discussion in Chapter 2 — there the "license to operate" becomes the criterion for choosing *which* stakeholders to take into account.

### The five ways operations impact the enterprise

The ability of OPM to impact the strategic success of any kind of enterprise is increasingly recognized, and it shows up on five levers (slide p. 13):

1. **Cost** — operations can reduce the costs of producing products and services by being efficient: the more productive the operation at transforming inputs into outputs, the lower the cost per unit of output.
2. **Revenue** — it can increase revenue by increasing customer satisfaction through quality, service and innovation: existing customers are more likely to be retained and new customers attracted if products are error-free and appropriately designed, if the operation is fast and responsive, keeps its delivery promises and can be flexible.
3. **Risk** — it can reduce the risk of operational failure, because well-designed and well-run operations are less likely to fail and, if they fail, should recover faster and with less disruption (**resilience**).
4. **Investment** —
> [!quote] 📖 p. 48
   > "It can ensure effective investment (capital employed) to produce its products and services. Eventually all businesses in the commercial world are judged by the return that they produce for their investors. This is a function of profit (the difference between costs and revenues) and the amount of money invested in the business's operations resources. (…) What is sometimes overlooked is the role of operations in reducing the investment required per unit of output. It does this by increasing the effective capacity of the operation and by being innovative in how it uses its physical resources."

5. **Capability** —
> [!quote] 📖 p. 48
   > "It can build capabilities that will form the basis for future innovation by building a solid base of operations skills and knowledge within the business. Every time an operation produces a product or a service it has the opportunity to accumulate knowledge about how that product or service is best produced. This accumulation of knowledge should be used as a basis for learning and improvement. (…) Conversely, if an operations function is simply seen as the mechanical and routine fulfilment of customer requests, then it is difficult to build the knowledge base that will allow future innovation."

💡 **The Bottega Veneta aside** (from your notes): why do big luxury brands stay in Italy instead of relocating? Because here they find a **concentration of capabilities** — productive knowledge embodied in people. It is the capability→competitiveness link made geographic, and it foreshadows the *inside-out* perspective of Chapter 2.

🎯 The principle to quote (flagged "need to mention for the exam" in your notes):

> **Operations Principle** — All operations should be expected to contribute to their enterprise by controlling costs, increasing revenue, reducing risk, making investment more effective and growing long-term capabilities.

## 1.6 Process-level performance: the five objectives

Judging operations at the *operational* level means asking how well the network of processes serves its internal and external customers. Five aspects, all affecting customer satisfaction and business competitiveness:

1. **Quality** — doing things *right*: error-free goods and services that are 'fit for their purpose'.
2. **Speed** — doing things *fast*: minimising the time between a customer asking and receiving the goods and services in full.
3. **Dependability** — doing things *on time*: keeping the delivery promises made to customers. *(Your notes' examples: Zara with its many collections per year, Amazon with its delivery volumes.)*
4. **Flexibility** — changing *what* you do or *how* you do it: adapting to unexpected circumstances, giving customers individual treatment, introducing new products or services.
5. **Cost** — doing things *cheaply*: producing at a cost that enables an appropriate market price while still allowing a return to the organisation (or, in a not-for-profit, good value to taxpayers or funders).

> 📖 **Operations Principle** (p. 49) — Operations and process performance at an operational level can be grouped together as **quality, speed, dependability, flexibility and cost**.

💡 Beware the "'operational' = not strategic" trap: the book warns explicitly not to think these aspects have little strategic significance — on the contrary, they all contribute to the success of the organization as a whole. Chapter 2 picks them up as the *performance objectives* that operations strategy must prioritise (order-winners and qualifiers).

## 1.7 The 4V diagnostic: "Are processes managed to reflect their operating circumstances?"

All processes differ, so all need managing differently. 'Technical' differences (skills, technologies) add to four **characteristics of demand and process** that have a systematic effect on how processes need to be managed — the **4Vs**:

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791192524661.webp]]

*🖼️ **The full 4V profile** — For each dimension, the low↔high continuum with the operational implications at the two extremes (matrix transcription below).*

The matrix transcribed from the slide (know this: **the expensive side is "high" for three Vs out of four; volume is the only V where the high end is the efficient one**):

| V              | "High" end → implications                                                                                                        | "Low" end → implications                                                                                                                 |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Volume**     | high repeatability → specialisation, systemisation, capital intensive, **low unit cost**                                         | low repetition → multi-task staff, less systemisation, **high unit cost**                                                                |
| **Variety**    | flexible, complex, matches customer needs, **high unit cost**                                                                    | well defined, routine, standardised, regular, **low unit cost**                                                                          |
| **Variation**  | capacity changes over time, anticipation, flexibility, cushion for the unexpected, **high unit cost**                            | stable, routine, predictable, high utilisation, **low unit cost**                                                                        |
| **Visibility** | short waiting tolerance, satisfaction governed by perception, customer contact skills, high received variety, **high unit cost** | time lag between production and consumption, standardised, low contact skills, high staff utilisation, centralisation, **low unit cost** |

Detail for each V (from the book via your notes):

- **Volume** — high volume = high repeatability: it makes sense for staff to specialize in the tasks they perform, to systemize activities with standard procedures, and to develop specialized technology for higher processing efficiency. Low volume = less repetition, staff performing a wide range of tasks. *(Vinelli's example: **Fincantieri** = low volume, one ship at a time; **McDonald's** = high volume, burgers really fast.)*
- **Variety** — high variety = a wide range of different activities, changing frequently; a wide range of skills and technologies; the extra complexity of matching customer requirements to appropriate products or services. High-variety processes are **invariably** more complex and costly than low-variety ones.
- **Variation in demand** — constant, predictable demand = resources geared to just the right level, everything plannable, no extra safety capacity → lower costs. Variable/unpredictable demand = resources adjusted over time, extra capacity cushion to absorb unexpected demand → higher costs.
- **Visibility** — indicates how much of the process is experienced directly by customers. Processes that act directly *on customers* (retail, healthcare) expose more of their activities; the customer interface needs managing → higher costs than low-visibility processes.

💡 **The expensive pair**: notice that three Vs out of four have the high cost on the "left" end (low volume, high variety, high variation, high visibility). In practice an operation's 4V *profile* almost always explains its unit cost — and that is why volume is the "good" V: McDonald's has high volume *and* low variety, and the two reinforce each other (from your notes: *"fast-food has high volume since it has low variety → standardised chain of work"*).

### Four profiles compared: retail banking

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791193009933.webp]]

*🖼️ **Figure 1.12** — Four Vs analysis for some retail banking processes: from personal banker/advisor (low volume, high variety, high variation, high visibility) to the bank branch, the call centre, down to app-based transactions (high volume, low variety, low variation, low visibility).*

The bank's app is not the branch gone digital: it is another *species* of process (high volume, all standardized). When the exam asks "the 4Vs of a company", always think: *which process, exactly?*

## 1.8 The operations manager's activities: the 4Ds

Operations management activities group into four broad categories:

- **Directing** the overall strategy of the operation — understanding its strategic purpose, how it is translated into reality, how innovation is incorporated into products and services, and how much of the total value-adding process should be kept in-house and outsourced.
- **Designing** the operation's processes — determining the physical form, shape and composition of operations and processes, together with the types of resources they contain.
- **Delivery** (planning and controlling) — planning and controlling the delivery of products and services from suppliers, through the total operation, to customers.
- **Developing** process performance — developing the capabilities of the processes to improve process performance.

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791193380459.webp]]

*🖼️ **Figure 1.14** — The extended model: data from suppliers, customers, internal and external sources becomes a **transforming resource** that, through analytics, informs operations decision-making in the four activities (direct, design, deliver, develop).*

💡 Figure 1.14 is the bridge to the present of the course: **data** enters the input-transformation-output model not just as a transformed resource (information being processed) but as a **transforming resource** — what *enables* decisions to be made (analytics at the centre of the diagram). Compare with Figure 1.4: the structure is the same, but now the information flows feed the 4Ds in real time.

## 1.9 💡 Analysis and links (Chapter 1)

1. **The common thread is transformation.** Definitions → input-transformation-output model → 4Vs → 4Ds: the whole chapter repeats that an operation is a machine turning resources into value, and that managing it well means understanding *what kind* of transformation it is (4Vs) and *what* directing/designing/delivering/developing require (4Ds).
2. **4Vs ↔ five objectives.** The unit cost the 4V matrix explains (1.7) is the same "cost" of the five objectives (1.6): the 4Vs say *why* a process is expensive, the objectives say *which levers* performance acts on. Visibility ↔ front/back office (1.4) is another internal bridge.
3. **Strategic vs operational.** The five enterprise-level impact levers (cost, revenue, risk, investment, capability) are the strategic "why"; the five process-level objectives are the operational "how". Chapter 2 welds them: process objectives become the *performance objectives* on which strategy is played.
4. **TBL → Chapter 2.** The "people, planet, profit" idea introduces the multi-stakeholder view of performance that Chapter 2 formalizes with stakeholders and CSR.
5. **OSCM → Chapter 4.** The fourth historical stage (supply chain) and the supply network level of Figure 1.7 open directly onto Chapter 4 (scope, structure, vertical integration: see `Summary SOSCM - Macchion.md`).

# Chapter 2 — Operations and strategic impact

*Base translations from your notes: **shareholders → azionisti**, **stakeholders → portatori di interessi**.*

The chapter answers one question: **how do operations produce competitive advantage, and how is an operations strategy formulated?** The book's answer is structured around four *perspectives* (top-down, bottom-up, outside-in, inside-out) that must be **reconciled** — and that reconciliation is the diagnostic question that closes the chapter.

## 2.1 Stakeholders and CSR

> [!quote] 📖 p. 70
> "All operations have stakeholders. They are the people and groups who have a legitimate interest in the operation's strategy. Some are internal (employees); others are external (customers, society or community groups, and a company's shareholders). External stakeholders may have a direct commercial relationship with the organisation (suppliers and customers); others may not (industry regulators). In not-for-profit operations, these stakeholder groups can overlap. (…) it is a responsibility of the operations function to understand the (often conflicting) objectives of its stakeholders and set its objectives accordingly. Yet, although all stakeholder groups, to different extents, will be interested in operations performance, they are likely to have very different views on which aspect of performance is important."

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791193646521.webp]]

*⚠️ 🖼️ Professor's slide on CSR (auto-transcription failed; from context: the definition of CSR and its link to the stakeholder perspective).*

**Corporate social responsibility (CSR)** is strongly related to the stakeholder perspective of operations performance: listening and responding to the needs of the company's stakeholders, including the requirements of sustainable development, and building good relationships with employees, suppliers and wider society.

🎯 **Operations Principle** — Operations strategy should take the requirements of significant stakeholders into account.

💡 The link to Chapter 1: stakeholders are the external face of the triple bottom line. Each group brings a different *metric* (shareholders → profit; employees → conditions; community → environmental impact): the operations strategy must navigate these trade-offs, and the TBL is the framework that holds them together.

## 2.2 The vision of operations contribution: Hayes & Wheelwright

An operations strategy should **articulate a vision** for the operations function's contribution to overall strategy: a clear statement of how operations intend to contribute value to the business. The common approach to summarize this progression is the **Hayes and Wheelwright four-stage model**:

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791193952041.webp]]

*🖼️ **Figure 2.2** — The H&W four-stage model: from "Stage 1 – correct the worst problems" ("holding the organisation back") to "Stage 4 – give an operations advantage" ("redefining industry expectations"). Axes: strategic impact (vertical) and operations capabilities (horizontal). The arrows between stages: implementing → supporting → driving strategy. (An identical second copy of this figure exists in the repo: `…-1791193953052.webp`.)*

- **Stage 1 — Internal neutrality.** The very poorest level of contribution: the other functions see operations as holding them back from competing effectively. Its goal (vision) is *to be ignored*: avoiding the bigger mistakes.
- **Stage 2 — External neutrality.** Operations start comparing themselves with similar companies in the outside market: adopt best practice ideas and norms of performance from others. Not setting the trend, just copying the best to achieve the best possible market position.
- **Stage 3 — Internally supportive.** They may not be better than competitors on every aspect of performance, but they are broadly up there. They gain a clear view of the company's competitive and strategic goals and develop appropriate operations resources to **excel in the areas where the company needs to compete effectively**. Vision: be clearly the very best in the market.
- **Stage 4 — Externally supportive.** Operations look to the long term and develop the operations-based capabilities that will be required to compete in future market conditions; they are creative, innovative and capable of adaptation as markets change. **Operations themselves drive the company strategy.** Vision: provide the foundation for competitive success.

💡 *"It's the apotheosis of the operations manager"* (from your notes): at Stage 4 it is no longer the business strategy telling operations what to do — it is the operation (its capabilities) *creating* the strategy. Examples to quote: **IKEA**, **Toyota** with the TPS (Toyota Production System), **Southwest Airlines** which invented a new way of flying — later copied (Ryanair).

## 2.3 The process of strategy: context, content, process

A classic distinction (popularised by the strategy academic **Andrew Pettigrew**, from his eight-year study of a single firm over time) separates three aspects of operations strategy:

- **Context** — the set of circumstances under which a strategy is formed: the environment in which strategy is shaped (sudden disruptions to supply, labour shortages, currency fluctuations, changes in ownership, technological disruption, new services offered by rivals). The answer to "what should we do?" is always *"it depends"* — on factors outside any individual organisation's control (geopolitical turbulence) and on internal ones (availability of the skills needed to implement a strategy).
- **Content** — the collection of strategic decisions (deliberate or by default) that shape and develop the long-term direction of the operation: capacity strategy, supply network strategy, technology strategy, and so on.
- **Process** — the way in which operations strategies are (or can be) formulated; it reflects what operations managers should do in practice.

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791195131226.webp|670]]

*🖼️ **The context–content–process framework** — Process (how decisions are made) ↔ Content (the decisions that form the strategy), all inside the Context (internal and external factors: organisational structure, culture, domestic and international market conditions).*

As **Andrew MacLennan** (strategy implementation expert, quoted in the book) says: *"The challenge of implementing strategy successfully is one that faces managers across the globe and in organizations of every kind. However, few organizations have discovered how to make strategy work reliably – the failure rate of planned strategies remains remarkably high."*

💡 Worth remembering when talking about "process": the three areas **overlap** — the reality of all decisions is that context, content and process contaminate each other. The figure shows it: the green context box envelops, process and content touch.

## 2.4 Top-down: correspondence and coherence

The **top-down** perspective starts from the business strategy and translates it down into functional strategy and operational decisions. Figure 2.4 shows the **two requirements** for the translation to work:

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791376701008.webp]]

*🖼️ **Figure 2.4** — Top to bottom: business strategy → functional strategies (HR, marketing, **operations**, finance, IT) → operations decision areas (product/service innovation, operations structure and scope, process design, supply chain management, capacity management…). Vertical = **correspondence** between levels; horizontal = **coherence** across functions and across decision areas.*

- **Correspondence** — the vertical match: the operations strategy must correspond to the business strategy, and the operational decisions to the operations strategy.
- **Coherence** — the horizontal consistency: the functional strategies must reinforce each other, and the operations decisions (process design ↔ capacity ↔ supply chain…) must not contradict one another.

💡 "All operations are done to achieve the planned goal" (from your notes): top-down works only if the vertical chain is intact and the horizontal one does not contradict itself. A capacity KPI rewarding maximum saturation can *kill* the flexibility the business strategy requires: the textbook example of broken coherence.

### Business model and operating model

Two concepts useful for understanding the top-down perspective:

> [!quote] 📖 p. 75

> "A 'business model' is the plan that is implemented by a company to generate revenue and make a profit (or fulfil its social objectives if a not-for-profit enterprise). It includes the various parts and organisational functions of the business, as well as the revenues it generates and the expenses it incurs. In other words, what a company does and how it makes money from doing it. More formally, it is 'a conceptual tool that contains a big set of elements and their relationships and allows [the expression of] the business logic of a specific firm. It is a description of the value a company offers to one or several segments of customers and of the architecture of the firm and its network of partners for creating, marketing, and delivering this value and relationship capital, to generate profitable and sustainable revenue streams.'"

The literature synthesis gives **nine common elements** of the business model:

1. The **value proposition** of what is offered to the market.
2. The **target customer segments** addressed by the value proposition.
3. The **communication and distribution channels** to reach customers.
4. The **relationships** established with customers.
5. The **core capabilities** needed to make the business model possible.
6. The **configuration of activities** to implement it.
7. The **partners** and their motivations for coming together.
8. The **revenue streams** generated (→ the revenue model).
9. The **cost structure** resulting from the business model.

The **operating model** instead describes *how the business model will be achieved*. Typical elements (no universal list exists):

- **Key performance indicators (KPIs)** — with the relative importance of performance objectives.

- **Core financial structure** — P&L, new investments and cash flow.

- **Nature of accountabilities** — for products, geographies, assets, etc.

- **Organisation structure** — sometimes expressed as capability areas rather than functional roles.

- **Systems and technologies**.

- **Processes, responsibilities and interactions**; key knowledge and competence.

Two clarifications from the book: (1) an operating model is **like an operations strategy, but applied across all functions and domains** of the organization; (2) the two models overlap — the main difference is that the operating model focuses on **how** the business strategy is achieved. And since operating models are rarely designed from first principles (some understood 'way of doing things' always already exists), they carry an element of implied **change/transformation**: hence the term **TOM (Target Operating Model)** — how the organization *should* operate in the future to achieve its objectives and make a success of its business model.

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791377142909.webp]]

*🖼️ **Figure 2.5** — The overlap between business model and operating model: the business model (business strategy + functional strategies) **sets the overall purpose and objectives**; the operating model (operational marketing/operations/finance/technology) **determines how the business model will be achieved**. At the bottom: the operating model's six questions (people skills and capabilities? performance measures? systems and technology? resources? business processes? responsibilities?).*

## 2.5 Outside-in: market requirements

The **outside-in** perspective starts from the market: what must the operation be able to do to win? (In your summary this part carries the label "2.3 OUTSIDE IN (MARKET REQUIREMENTS)".)

### The exact meaning of performance objectives differs between operations

> 📖 **Operations Principle** (p. 80) — The interpretation of the five performance objectives will differ between different operations.

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791377607658.webp]]

*🖼️ **Table 2.2** — The same five objectives translated into concrete aspects for two very different operations: an insurance company (service) and a steel plant (manufacturing). E.g. quality: "professionalism of staff" (insurance) vs "percentage of products conforming to their specification" (steel).*

💡 The table's value: the objectives are **universal but their measurement is local**. "Speed" for an insurer is the call centre response time and fast quotation decisions; for a steel plant it is the lead time from order to delivery. In the exam, when asked about the five objectives, the ten-out-of-ten move is to *instantly translate them into the context* of the company you are discussing.

### Order-winners and qualifiers

> [!quote] 📖 p. 81

> "**Order-winners** are those things that directly and significantly contribute to winning business. They are regarded by customers as key reasons for purchasing the product or service. Raising performance in an order-winner will either result in more business or improve the chances of gaining more business. (…) **Qualifiers** may not be the major competitive determinants of success, but are important in another way. They are those aspects of competitiveness where the operation's performance has to be above a particular level just to be considered by the customer. Performance below this 'qualifying' level of performance may disqualify the operation from being considered by customers. But any further improvement above the qualifying level is unlikely to gain the company much competitive benefit. Qualifiers are those things that are generally expected by customers. Being great at them is unlikely to excite customers, but being bad at them can disadvantage the competitive position of the operation."

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791377748814.webp]]

*⚠️ 🖼️ **Figure 2.6** — The order-winner vs qualifier curves (auto-transcription failed): on the horizontal axis the operation's performance, on the vertical its contribution to competitiveness. The order-winner curve rises steadily and significantly as performance improves; the qualifier curve is flat above the qualifying threshold and penalising below it.*

- **Order-winners**: raising the level → more business (or better chances of getting it).

- **Qualifiers**: below a threshold you are *excluded* from the customer's consideration; above the threshold, further improvement brings little appreciable competitive benefit.

🎯 **Operations Principle** (p. 80) — Operations strategy should reflect the requirements of the business's markets.

💡 **"Yesterday's order-winner is today's qualifier"** (from your notes): the customer gets used to it — what once won orders is now taken for granted, and the qualifying threshold rises. It is the dynamic by which *no operational advantage is permanent*: it connects directly to the efficient frontier (section 2.7) and to the need to develop capabilities (inside-out).

### The product/service life cycle influence on performance objectives

One way of generalising the market requirements is to link them to the **life cycle** of the products or services — introduction, growth, maturity, decline:

- **Introduction** — the product or service is likely to offer something new in design or performance; market uncertainty is high → the operation needs **flexibility** (to cope with changes) and **quality** (to maintain product/service performance).

- **Growth** — standardised designs emerge that allow the operation to supply the rapidly growing market; keeping up with demand through rapid and dependable response while maintaining quality helps keep market share as competition starts to increase.

- **Maturity** — demand levels off, the market is dominated by a few larger companies with standardised designs; competition probably emphasises price or value for money → operations must **get costs down** (with dependable supply) to maintain profits or allow price-cutting, or both.

- **Decline** — sales decline; a residual market may remain, but if capacity lags demand the market is dominated by price competition → **cost-cutting remains important**.

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791379072444.webp]]

*🖼️ **Figure 2.7** — The effects of the life cycle on the operation and its process performance objectives. Key rows: **customers** (innovators → early adopters → bulk of market → laggards), **likely order-winners** (product/service characteristics, performance or novelty → availability of quality products/services → low price + dependable supply → low price), **likely qualifiers** (quality, range → price, range → range, quality → dependable supply), **dominant process performance objectives** (flexibility, quality → speed, dependability, quality → cost, dependability → cost).*

💡 **The most profitable link in the whole chapter** (perfect exam material): the life cycle is a **drift of the 4V profile**. At introduction you are in low volume, high variety, high variation (uncertain market) — and that is why the *dominant process performance objectives* are flexibility and quality. In maturity the profile has slid towards high volume, low variety, low variation — and the dominant objectives become cost and dependability. The 4Vs (Ch. 1) explain *how the process changes*; Figure 2.7 says *what becomes important*; order-winners/qualifiers say *why* (what makes the customer buy at that stage).

## 2.6 Inside-out: operations capabilities

The **inside-out** perspective is the mirror image of outside-in: instead of asking "what does the market want?", it asks "**what can we do better than anyone else?**" It starts from the operation's accumulated capabilities (p. 48, Ch. 1: the accumulation of knowledge that becomes the basis for future innovation — see also the Bottega Veneta case) and works back up to which markets/strategies those capabilities make possible.

💡 The link to the H&W model: **Stage 4** ("operations drive strategy") is inside-out taken to the extreme — TPS and IKEA did not copy the market, they *created* a model starting from their own operational capabilities. And the link to Chapter 1: capabilities are the fifth strategic impact lever (capability → future innovation).

## 2.7 The four perspectives together (and the diagnostic question)

### The diagnostic question

> **Are the four perspectives of operations strategy reconciled?**

In your summary this section carries the label "2.6 Diagnostic question". The challenge: top-down, bottom-up, outside-in and inside-out are not alternatives to choose from — they all provide constraints and opportunities, and a good operations strategy **reconciles** them. The book shows it with the **Micraytech** case:

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791380476922.webp]]

*🖼️ **Figure 2.9** — The four perspectives applied to Micraytech: **top-down** (from the group: building corporate capability in high-tech; from the business: growth through systems-based innovations), **bottom-up** (customers confused by continual system innovation, costs increasing → experiment with 'modular' design), **inside-out** (not all modules can be integrated → need to buy-in technical capabilities for interface modules → develop supply capability), **outside-in** (the growth market is 'integrated metrology systems'; maintain position in 'individual metrology devices'; maintain technical skills, increase software development, data exchange and client liaison skills). At the centre: the reconciled strategy — "operations develops modular-based hi-tech systems that provide highly customised flexibility and innovation at relatively low cost through close relationships with both clients and suppliers".*

💡 Read the central box the way you read a well-made strategy: it contains *all four* perspectives (modular design = the bottom-up answer to customer complaints; customised flexibility = inside-out capability; low cost = the outside-in market requirement; close relationships = the top-down group direction). No perspective is sacrificed: that is the "reconciliation" of the diagnostic question.

### The operations strategy matrix

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791380599088.webp]]

*🖼️ **Figure 2.10** — **The operations strategy matrix** for a parcel delivery courier: rows are the performance objectives (with importance stars: quality of service ★★★, range of services ★, delivery dependability ★★★, innovation ★★, cost ★★), columns are the strategic decisions split into **design / delivery / development**. Each cell = a decision serving that objective (e.g. delivery dependability: design → location of depots; delivery → demand–capacity management; development → vehicle maintenance programme).*

💡 Note the matrix structure: the columns are the 4Ds of Chapter 1 (design, deliver, develop — with 'direct' implicit in the design column), the rows are the five performance objectives. It is the **geometric bridge** between the two chapters: the operations strategy *is* the set of intersections. If the exam asks "what is an operations strategy?", the strongest answer is: *"a coherent set of design/delivery/development decisions, weighted by the importance of the performance objectives the market requires".*

### Reconciling market and capabilities over time: the line of fit

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791380753325.webp]]

*⚠️ 🖼️ **Figure 2.11 — the 'line of fit' model** (auto-transcription failed; from context: reconciling market requirements with operations capabilities **over time**). The idea: the strategy must sit on the "line of fit" between what the market rewards (order-winners) and what the operation can do (dominant performance objectives) — and realign itself when the two series drift (e.g. through the life cycle).*

### The efficient frontier

![[Summary SOSCM - Vinelli-1791380934203.webp]]

*🖼️ **Figure 2.12** — The efficient frontier: (a) horizontal axis cost efficiency, vertical axis variety; the frontier is the boundary of maximum achievable combinations. Point X is *inside* the frontier: you can improve one dimension without sacrificing the other (effectiveness). (b) an operational breakthrough pushes point B to B1: the frontier shifts and what was 'best' becomes the new baseline.*

Two lessons from the figure:

1. **Effectiveness ≠ frontier**: an operation *inside* the frontier (X) can improve service quality AND cost together — that is not a trade-off, that is underperformance.

2. **Trade-offs exist ON the frontier** — but the frontier itself moves: operational innovation (B→B1) redefines what is possible, and the old frontier becomes the new minimum standard.

💡 **Close the circle**: the shifting frontier is the mechanism behind "yesterday's order-winner is today's qualifier" (2.5) — when an operation shifts the frontier (SWA with low-cost flying, Toyota with the TPS), the market raises the threshold and everyone else must chase. H&W Stage 4, order-winners and the efficient frontier are **three tellings of the same phenomenon**: operations do not support strategy, they *generate* it.

## 2.8 💡 Analysis and links (Chapter 2)

1. **The four perspectives as forces, not options.** Top-down (business strategy ↓), bottom-up (operational experience ↑), outside-in (market ←), inside-out (capabilities →): real strategy emerges from the tension between the four (Figure 2.9). A strategy that disowns any one perspective is fragile: pure top-down = strategy that cannot be executed; pure outside-in = me-too; pure inside-out = a solution looking for a problem; pure bottom-up = incrementalism without direction.

2. **Chapter 2 is Chapter 1 made strategic.** The five performance objectives (1.6) become the rows of the operations strategy matrix (2.10); the 4Ds (1.8) become the columns; capabilities (1.5) become the inside-out; stakeholders/TBL (1.5) become the starting point (2.1). Study them together.

3. **The life cycle as translator.** Order-winners and qualifiers are static until you project them in time: Figure 2.7 is the 'drift' of order-winners over time, and the 4Vs (1.7) are the operational mechanism that makes it unavoidable.

4. **Business model vs operating model** is the corporate version of correspondence/coherence: the business model says *what* and *for whom* (9 elements), the operating model says *how* (KPIs, accountabilities, systems, processes) — and the TOM is the operating model as a *transformation target*.

## The "Operations Principles" of the chapters (the book's boxes)

1. Operations should judge themselves on the **triple bottom line** principle of 'people, planet and profit' (p. 47).

2. All operations should be expected to contribute to their enterprise by **controlling costs, increasing revenue, reducing risk, making investment more effective and growing long-term capabilities** (Ch. 1).

3. Operations and process performance at an operational level can be grouped together as **quality, speed, dependability, flexibility and cost** (p. 49).

4. Operations strategy should take the requirements of **significant stakeholders** into account (Ch. 2).

5. The interpretation of the five performance objectives **will differ between different operations** (p. 80).

6. Operations strategy should reflect the requirements of the **business's markets** (p. 80).
